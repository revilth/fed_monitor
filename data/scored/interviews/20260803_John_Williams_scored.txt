GROWTH
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Economic activity is expanding at a solid pace" [Jul 29 stmt, frozen from
   Jun 17]
  → Williams version: "Growth has been solid, but not more than solid."

Own language:
  "I mean, GDP growth over the last year was 2%, it's a little less than that in
   the first half of this year. So we're not seeing the economy show any signs
   of overheating in terms of labor market, in terms of growth. We're seeing
   economy that's growing more or less at trend"
  → Two changes of emphasis vs. July 15. (a) The qualifier "but not more than
    solid" caps the characterization — July 15 had growth "running modestly
    above my estimate of its potential rate of 2 percent"; here growth is "more
    or less at trend" and H1 is "a little less" than 2%. (b) The purpose of the
    growth read has flipped from descriptive to argumentative: it is deployed as
    the first plank of the case AGAINST hiking ("no signs of overheating").
  → The July 15 compositional framing (AI/investment strength offsetting housing,
    federal spending and energy drag) is not repeated; Derby did not ask about
    composition, so this is not a withdrawal. [Type J caveat applies.]

Shift vs. prior speech (Jul 15):  DOVISH LEAN — "modestly above potential"
  downgraded to "at trend" and "not more than solid."
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Jul 15):      DOVISH LEAN — same comparison; July 15 is
  both the prior and the pre-FOMC baseline.

LABOR MARKET
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "the unemployment rate has changed little" [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Williams version: "the unemployment rate has been near constant for about
    a year."

Own verdict:
  "from my perspective, the labor market is, been stable."
  → Verbatim continuity with the July 15 verdict ("solid and stable") minus
    "solid." Given the interview format, do not read the dropped adjective as a
    downgrade; the substantive claim — stability, no deterioration — is intact.

Own qualifier / addition:
  "we're not seeing the economy show any signs of overheating in terms of labor

    inflation") restated in argumentative form. It is the direct rebuttal to the
    dissenters' demand-side attribution: if labor is neither tight nor
    inflationary, the case for a pre-emptive hike loses its channel.

INFLATION
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Inflation remains elevated relative to the Committee's 2 percent goal, in
   part reflecting supply shocks including energy" [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Williams version: the same three-category decomposition he has used since
    June 25 — (1) tariffs, (2) the Middle East conflict / energy, (3) "the
    effects of very strong demand related to AI."

Own language — KEY:
  (1) TARIFFS — peak claim RETAINED and strengthened:
      "probably the vast majority or large part of the effects of the tariffs
       that are in place have now passed through into prices … we should not see
       large effects from tariffs on inflation rates in the coming months
       anymore." New tariffs are "a relatively modest increase in the average
       tariff rate. So it doesn't really change my view."
      → July 15 said the direct effects "appear to have in large part played
        out." Substantively the same claim, now stated with less hedging.

  (2) ENERGY — peak claim RETAINED but the level marked UP:
      "Moderating somewhat, or basically having essentially already peaked."
      AND: "definitely relative to some of my earlier comments, oil prices are
       higher, and I think the path of future path of oil prices is also
       somewhat higher."
      → This is the one explicitly acknowledged hawkish revision in the
        interview. July 15's futures-based call was that energy prices "have
        likely peaked and will come down closer to levels seen before the
        initial closure of the Strait of Hormuz." He now keeps the shape of the
        path (peaked, declining) but raises its level. He also disclaims
        confidence: "I'm not putting a great deal of certainty or confidence in
        any particular forecast of energy prices."

  (3) AI DEMAND — explicitly DE-RANKED as an inflation driver:
      "It's not a big driver of inflation right now, but is clearly something
       that we're seeing, certain categories of goods being pushed up by the
       very very strong demand that's right now at pace and supply."
      → HIGH SIGNAL. July 15 listed AI demand as one of three co-equal drivers
        and described "a race between available supply and surging demand …
        sharp increases in the prices of semiconductors, power transformers."
        "Not a big driver of inflation right now" is a demotion. It puts Williams
        directly opposite Cook (Aug 5, AI capex reframed as a demand-side
        inflation impulse) and opposite Hammack's dissent attribution
        ("inflationary pressures coming from the demand side"). This is the
        clearest Troika-vs-dissenter divide in the post-July-29 corpus.

  RISK LEVEL — unchanged:
      "It's about … similar to the June FOMC over these six weeks. My view on
       the economic outlook actually hasn't changed that much more generally."

  BASE CASE — 2028 target date REAFFIRMED, with the conditions stated:
      "assuming that energy prices have basically peaked … assuming that the
       tariff effects have also peaked … and assuming that we keep the economy
       … in a good macroeconomic balance," then "goods inflation can get back to
       rates that are kind of more normal around zero, or even slightly
       negative," core services ex-housing gradually comes down, and inflation
       reaches 2% "on a sustained basis by 2028."
      → Same 2028 date as June 25 and July 15. Housing/shelter disinflation
        again named as the standing background force.

  WHAT HE IS ACTUALLY WATCHING:
      "I am quite honestly focused quite a bit on what are we seeing in the core
       inflation data over the next several months, and is that consistent with
       a kind of a run rate of inflation moving towards 2%."
      → Names CORE, over "the next several months," as his own test. This is the
        closest thing to a decision rule he offers, and it is a multi-month test
        — implicitly a high bar for a September move in either direction.

VERIFICATION NOTE: Williams does NOT repeat the July 15 headline formulation
  "inflation has peaked" as a standalone claim in this interview. He asserts the
  peak for the tariff and energy components specifically, and the disinflation
  path in forecast form ("my forecast personally is for inflation to come down in
  the second half of this year and come down further next year"). Do not report
  the aggregate peak claim as restated verbatim; report it as intact in substance
  and unrepeated in form.

Shift vs. prior speech (Jul 15):  MIXED, net UNCHANGED — hawkish on the energy
  level (marked up, explicitly), dovish on AI (demoted from co-equal driver to
  "not a big driver"), unchanged on tariffs, risk level and the 2028 date.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Jul 15):      same as above.

MONETARY POLICY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "The Committee decided to maintain the target range … in support of the
   Federal Reserve's dual mandate." [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Williams version: "I strongly supported the decision of the committee, and
    there was strong support for that."

Own language — THE ANTI-HIKE CASE, stated affirmatively:
  Asked directly why the Fed did not hike given the dissents, Williams gave the
  majority's reasoning on the record for the first time:
  "the labor market is, been stable. Growth has been solid, but not more than
   solid … we're not seeing the economy show any signs of overheating … the big
   drivers of inflation … really have been from these specific sets of
   circumstances around the tariffs, around some of the uncertainty, geopolitical
   events, and … the conflict in the Middle East. In my base forecast, we should
   see those factors diminish … And I think monetary policy currently is well
   positioned in where we are today to support that disinflationary path."
  → Structure of the argument: (a) no overheating channel; (b) inflation is
    driven by identifiable, fading, supply-side specifics; (c) policy is already
    correctly positioned for the resulting disinflation. This is the majority's
    supply-shock attribution defended explicitly against the dissenters'
    demand-side attribution.

  Forward-looking framing as the dividing line:
  "that's why the case is … really very forward looking. It's not looking
   backwards, but it's looking, where is the economy today? Where is the economy
   going … Given that monetary policy takes time to have its full effects."
  → [Inference: this reframes the dissenters' "inflation has been above target
    for too long" as a backward-looking argument. Not stated as a criticism of
    any colleague; Williams is careful to speak "from my own perspective."]

  THE CONDITIONAL — new in form, symmetric in framing, hike-compatible:
  "if the economy is not on a trajectory that will bring inflation back down to
   2% … it would absolutely be appropriate to act to get us on a trajectory that
   does bring inflation back to 2%. I don't think now, what that situation might
   be or how, what it would be."
  → Elicited by a direct question about higher inflation readings, so the topic
    is Derby's — but the formulation is considered, not an aside. Williams had NO
    such conditional on July 15, where "well positioned" carried the stance
    unqualified. Direction of the conditional is unnamed ("act," not "raise"),
    but the question was explicitly about hiking, so it functions as an
    acknowledgment of the hawkish tail. It is materially weaker than Cook's
    "prepared to act if disinflation does not appear soon" (Jul 15) and far
    weaker than Waller's Jul 13 named-trigger conditional.

  ON MARKET PRICING OF SEPTEMBER — declines to validate:
  "I don't opine on whether they're right or wrong because I don't know."
  And on long-term yields doing the tightening: "I never like the phrase that
  the markets are doing the work for us because I know that we have to do the
  work ourselves … those are factors that affect conditions, and we're aware of
  that. But it doesn't tell us what to do."
  → Notable in the Warsh regime, whose stated premise is that policy should lean
    on market signals. Williams accepts markets as INPUT and rejects them as
    INSTRUCTION. [Inference: a boundary being drawn around the Chair's
    market-signal doctrine, stated in his own institutional terms rather than as
    disagreement.]

  ON THE FORWARD-GUIDANCE PULLBACK — endorses it, and bounds it:
  "the committee strongly supported this in the June meeting, and I did too …
   that was exactly the right call because the uncertainties are such that we
   didn't have that confidence or conviction." But: "I don't see that as saying,
   well forward guidance is never useful," citing the zero lower bound and
   Greenspan 2003–04 as periods when it served. "So right now, I don't see
   forward guidance as being helpful to achieving our goals."
  → Conditional endorsement, not a doctrinal conversion. Guidance is a tool for
    when the committee and markets diverge; today they do not diverge enough to
    need it.

Cut signal status: ABSENT — unchanged since the April 16 retraction.
Hike signal status: ABSENT as a signal; a direction-unnamed conditional is now
  present where none existed on July 15.

Shift vs. prior speech (Jul 15):  HAWKISH LEAN, marginal — stance formula
  ("well positioned") unchanged, but a conditional-to-act now sits alongside it.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Jul 15):      same.

SUMMARY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Net signal:    NEUTRAL (hold-anchored), with the majority's anti-hike case
               articulated on the record for the first time. Williams is not
               moving; he is defending. The interview's value is not a stance
               change — the stance formula is verbatim July 15 — but that a
               Troika member states WHY the nine did not hike, plank by plank,
               two days after three regional presidents said publicly that they
               should have. On the substantive dividing line — what is causing
               the inflation — he demotes AI demand to "not a big driver," which
               is the direct negation of Hammack's and Logan's demand-side
               attribution and, later, of Cook's August 5 reframing.

               Committee positioning implied as of July 31:
               → Williams (Troika): supply-shock attribution; no overheating;
                 policy already correctly positioned; watching CORE over
                 "several months"
               → Hammack / Kashkari / Logan (Just Voters, dissenting): demand-
                 side attribution; policy not appropriately restrictive
               → Cook (Just Voter, majority): moved TOWARD the demand-side
                 attribution on August 5 (AI capex as inflation impulse)
               → Warsh (Chair, Troika): no attribution offered publicly

    contested within the majority by Cook). Resolution should be visible in the
    July minutes (Aug 19) — how the participants' AI-capex discussion is

  • "not more than solid" (growth) — a capped characterization from the Troika's
    number two. Stage: DISPERSED. Watch for it in Jefferson's next appearance.
  • "if the economy is not on a trajectory … it would absolutely be appropriate

  • "I never like the phrase that the markets are doing the work for us" —
    institutional, not directional. Relevant to the Warsh market-signal doctrine
    and to Jackson Hole (Aug 27–29).
