GROWTH
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
[Interviewer-initiated, via the bond-yield question; growth is not treated
separately.]

FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Economic activity is expanding at a solid pace ... Productivity growth and
   capital investment are strong." [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Williams: "what's driving it in large part is higher yields is really a
    strong U.S. economy and a strong economic outlook fueled by big investments
    in AI and data centers and technology in general."

Own language:
  "a lot of this is being driven by very strong demand for investment that is
   pushing yields up. And that is affecting of course sectors that are interest
   sensitive in more negative ways. So some crowding out there."
  → "Strong" is his adjective; the statement's is "solid." He uses the
    strength to explain the yield rise, and concedes a crowding-out cost to
    rate-sensitive sectors, attributed to the investment surge rather than to
    policy.

Shift vs. prior speech (Aug 3):   HAWKISH LEAN on the read — August 3 had
  "Growth has been solid, but not more than solid" and "more or less at
  trend"; September 2 has "a strong U.S. economy and a strong economic
  outlook." The strength is not used to argue for tightening.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Jul 15):      HAWKISH LEAN — July 15: "solid and on
  trend," with weak sectors (housing, federal spending, energy drag on
  households) named as offsets; September 2 names only the AI-investment leg.

INFLATION
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Inflation remains elevated relative to the Committee's 2 percent goal, in
   part reflecting supply shocks that have driven price increases in certain
   sectors, including energy." [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Williams: "the tariffs and the effects of the conflict in the Middle East
    in terms of energy prices and ... broader commodity prices are the biggest
    drivers of inflation. That's driving inflation above our 2% longer run
    goal."
  → The statement's supply-shock attribution is echoed and put first. Among
    post-FOMC speakers this is the clearest defense of that clause: Warsh
    (Aug 28) did not use it; Waller (Sep 3) says the shocks are no longer a
    source of "ongoing" pressure; Hammack and Logan (Jul 31) attributed
    pressure to demand.

Own language — KEY:
  "Core inflation today is running around 3.3%. A big chunk of that, you know,
   inflation above 2% is from the effects of the higher energy prices and
   tariffs. There's there's still some effects of higher some effects of higher
   service inflation too."
  "when I think about inflation today, it is important to like disaggregate the
   data, look at the different pieces and think through that."
  "so far, the data is that we're not seeing kind of second round effects or
   broadening of the tariffs. We're not seeing unusual broadening of the effects
   of higher energy prices. So and we're seeing well-anchored inflation
   expectations and and pretty contained compensation growth. So I am actually,
   you know, seeing inflation, the trend in inflation moving slowly down as
   some of the effects of the tariffs can move into the rear view mirror. But
   we've got to be data dependent."
  → The test he applies is attribution and broadening: are the tariff and
    energy effects producing second-round effects? Answer: no. Warsh (Aug 28)
    also said "disaggregate" and applied a breadth count (54% of the PCE
    basket above 3%) — same verb, different operation, opposite verdict
    ("do not tell me that underlying trends have meaningfully improved" vs.
    "the trend in inflation moving slowly down").
  AI: "the AI investment, which is showing up in some of the inflation data too
   in terms of semiconductor prices and software prices" — listed among
   factors to analyze. August 3 called AI demand "not a big driver of
   inflation right now"; the characterization here is descriptive, neither a
   promotion nor a demotion.

Shift vs. prior speech (Aug 3):   DOVISH LEAN — August 3 gave the disinflation
  as forecast ("my forecast personally is for inflation to come down in the
  second half of this year"; "we should see those factors diminish").
  September 2 reports it as observed: "I am actually ... seeing ... the trend
  in inflation moving slowly down." Tariffs "in the rear view mirror" is new.
  The no-broadening claim is carried from August 3.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Jul 15):      UNCHANGED — July 15 called the peak
  ("inflation has peaked and should edge down in coming quarters"); September
  2 describes the decline as under way. The peak call is confirmed, not
  extended; no updated forecast is given.

MONETARY POLICY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "The Committee decided to maintain the target range ... in support of the
   Federal Reserve's dual mandate." [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Williams: "I obviously supported our decision not to raise rates in the
    last meeting. I think, you know, at that time, it was let's get some more
    data. The labor market is solid. It's stable."
  "The Committee will deliver price stability." [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Williams: "it's our job to ensure that we ensure price stability. And most
    importantly right now is to get inflation back to 2% on a sustained basis.
    That's job number one."

Own language — bond yields and financial conditions:
  "We're not seeing it in terms of inflation compensation. We're seeing it
   mostly in real rates. So I think much of this is really about with a strong
   economy, you expect ... the cost of funding, this investment tends to go
   up."
  "So I think it's not really about financial conditions affecting the
   economy. It's more about the economy affecting financial conditions."
  → Yields are read as an outcome of strong investment demand, not as a
    policy input. Note the decomposition matches the minutes ("Nominal
    Treasury yields rose, driven by increases in real yields"). Warsh on July
    29 used the same real-rate fact to describe "a material tightening"; on
    August 28 he said the level of conditions is not restrictive. Williams's
    claim is about causation, not restrictiveness — the two are not the same
    proposition, but they point to different policy conclusions from the same
    yield move.
  Asked whether "the market [can] do the work for the Fed":
  "That's job number one. So that's our job. And you know, nobody else can do
   that for us. That's that's something that we'll have to do through our the
   decisions we make over the next few years."
  → Same position as August 3 ("I never like the phrase that the markets are
    doing the work for us because I know that we have to do the work
    ourselves"), stated more firmly and as institutional responsibility.
  On markets as input: "those are inputs into my thinking. Obviously, we study
   them very carefully. But at the end of the day, we look at the totality of
   the data, what it means for the economic outlook, the risks to achieving our
   goals and come to our best judgment about what policy needs to be."

Own language — the path:
  "make sure that we're on a path that will bring inflation back down to 2% on
   a sustained basis over the next year or two. Now, I do think that, you know,
   it's important not only that we are on that path, but we are on that path to
   to do that, you know, in, in the foreseeable future. So that's, that's
   going" [clip ends]
  → The bar he sets is being on the path AND getting there "in the foreseeable
    future" / "over the next year or two." The sentence that would have
    completed the thought is not in the published clip.

SUMMARY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Net signal:    DOVISH LEAN (mild) — hold-anchored
               The stance is unchanged; the movement is in the inflation
               read, from forecast (Aug 3) to observed ("I am actually ...
               seeing ... the trend in inflation moving slowly down"), with
               tariffs "in the rear view mirror" and no second-round effects.
               He defends the statement's supply-shock attribution more
               directly than any other post-FOMC speaker, reads the yield
               rise as a consequence of strong investment rather than a
               policy input, and rejects the idea that the market can do the
               Fed's job. Growth is characterized as "strong," but the
               strength is used to explain yields, not to argue for
               tightening. No September signal survives in the clip.

               Position relative to the Committee: within the Troika, his
               inflation verdict ("moving slowly down") is the opposite of
               the Chair's five days earlier ("do not tell me that underlying
               trends have meaningfully improved"), reached by a different
               test (attribution/broadening vs. breadth). On the trend he is
               with Waller (Sep 3: "signs of disinflation," three-month core
               falling), by a different route. On yields and the stance he
               does not join the not-restrictive group (Warsh, Schmid, the
               dissenters); he reframes the question rather than answering
               it. He is the FOMC Vice Chair; the Vice Chair of the Board
               (Jefferson) has not spoken since before July 29.

Language watch:
  • "It's more about the economy affecting financial conditions" — yields as
    outcome, not input. Stage: DISPERSED (first use). Watch Jefferson and
    Board members.
  • "Nobody else can do that for us" — anti-substitution, second iteration
    (Aug 3: "we have to do the work ourselves"). Stage: DISPERSED (Williams).
  • "The trend in inflation moving slowly down" / tariffs "in the rear view
    mirror" — Stage: EMERGING (minutes "several participants ... pass-through
    ... now largely complete"; Waller Sep 3 "largely passed through").
  • "Disaggregate the data" — used by Warsh (Aug 28) and Williams (Sep 2) for
    different operations. Watch which definition of underlying inflation the
    September minutes carry.

Analyst flag:  YES
  → (1) The Troika now carries two public verdicts on underlying inflation
    that point in opposite directions (Warsh: breadth, not improving;
    Williams: attribution, moving down). Neither is wrong on its own terms and
    no single print reconciles them. Which framework the September statement's
    inflation sentence and the SEP discussion adopt is the tell.
  → (2) The clip ends mid-sentence at the point where a path/September
    remark would most plausibly follow ("on that path ... in the foreseeable
    future. So that's, that's going"). A full-tape or rush-transcript
    recovery is worth one targeted attempt before the meeting.
  → (3) Williams is the clearest remaining public defender of the statement's
    supply-shock clause. If it survives into the September statement, this
    is where the support is; if it is dropped, note who lost the argument.
  → (4) First Williams communication since the −23K July payroll, and it does
    not engage it ("solid ... stable"). Not asked; do not over-read.
