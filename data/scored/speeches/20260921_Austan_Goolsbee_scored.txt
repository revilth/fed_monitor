GROWTH
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Growth is treated as a demand-overheating question, not a level question.
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "domestic spending has been resilient ... capital investment is robust"
  [Sep 16 stmt]
  → Speaker version: not echoed as a description; he runs the same facts
    as a risk — "any evidence that AI data center construction is
    spilling out of its own lane and raising aggregate output beyond what
    the economy can absorb."
Own language:
  "the bigger the hype the bigger the risk of short-run overheating ...
   we're going to invest and spend out of our stock market wealth before
   the capacity has actually expanded, then that could overheat it today.
   So I'm still worried about that." [Q&A]
  → The June 25 productivity-overheating frame, absent on July 14, is
    back and now sits inside the policy argument: AI-adjacent inflation
    is one of the two things he names as possible "old-fashioned demand
    overheating."
  Fiscal (asked): "part of aggregate demand ... in an accounting sense
   much of that impact is what's the delta from last year." Declines to
   editorialize; rejects the "cut to make debt issuance cheaper" argument
   as "the canonical reason why you want monetary independence."
Shift vs. prior speech (Jul 14):  hawkish lean — overheating risk
  reinstated as a live concern and tied to a policy consequence ("if
  demand overheats, there is no ambiguity about how the Fed needs to
  respond").
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Jul 14):      same document — hawkish lean.

LABOR MARKET
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Job gains have kept pace with the workforce, and the unemployment rate
   has changed little." [Sep 16 stmt]
  → Speaker version: no echo of the statement line; the labor read is
    reduced to the unemployment rate and used as the premise for the
    inflation argument.
Own verdict:
  "If the unemployment rate is barely 4% and core inflation is above three
   on an extended basis, there's not a conflict of mandates. There's just
   an inflation problem." [Q&A, to a question on "timelier"]
  "I know right now we do not have an employment problem and we do have
   an inflation problem." [Q&A, on the stance]
  → The standing idiom "stable without being good" (May 12 / Jun 25 /
    Jul 14) does NOT appear. The labor market is no longer characterized
    at all; it is invoked only as absence of a problem. That is the
    labor-side cover for patience being withdrawn in his own voice — the
    same move the SEP made (unemployment upside-risk count 7 → 0).
Own framework:
  The four rates-based measures and the low-hire/low-fire paradox are not
  mentioned in either the remarks or the Q&A.
Shift vs. prior speech (Jul 14):  hawkish lean — from "stable without
  being good" plus the hiring-rate-at-recession-lows worry to "we do not
  have an employment problem"; the qualifier is gone.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Jul 14):      same document — hawkish lean.

INFLATION
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Inflation remains elevated. Today's policy action will support a
   timelier return to the Committee's 2 percent goal." [Sep 16 stmt]
  → Speaker version: the DELETED clause is what he engages, not the
    retained one. The statement dropped "in part reflecting supply shocks
    ... including energy"; the whole speech is an argument for why
    supply-shock attribution no longer licenses looking through. He
    supplies the reasoning the statement omitted.
Own language — KEY (the framework):
  "My argument is that in this new environment, there are some supply
   shocks that central banks should not simply look through—namely, the
   persistent ones."
  "If a central bank commits to hitting 2% inflation in the medium term
   and commits to not respond to supply shocks, then a repeated or
   persistent supply shock to inflation means one of those two
   commitments can't hold up."
  "these days, when you see a large supply shock, it's usually more
   accurate to assume it will be persistent than to assume it will
   quickly go away."
  "My presumption—especially for big shocks—is that they will end up
   lasting longer than we initially think."
  → A presumption of persistence is a reversal of his own July 14 tariff
    line ("tariffs should be a one-time increase in prices but not a
    lasting inflation rate increase. That should be one and done"). Here:
    "Tariffs have been nothing like the stylized textbook example of a
    one-time price increase—they've instead followed a pattern of
    repeated escalation." He has changed his mind on the component he
    used to look through.
Own language — the peak-date slippage:
  "forecasters have spent more than a year pushing back the date when
   inflation was supposed to peak and start falling. Originally it was
   supposed to happen in Q4 2025. Then Q1 2026. Then Q2, then Q3, then
   Q4—and now sometime in 2027. That's not a comforting pattern."
  "We need evidence that these shocks are actually fading, or it's hard
   to see a credible path back to 2% inflation—and harder still to
   justify continuing to look through them."
  → On July 14 he was "heartened" by one benign CPI print but refused to
    bank it ("one month is no months"). Two months later the discount
    rule has resolved against the data: the summer improvement did not
    become the "several months" he required.
Own language — what he watches (the demand-side tell):
  "I'm especially attuned to elevated inflation in service-sector
   industries, and to any evidence that AI data center construction is
   spilling out of its own lane ... Either could be signs of old-fashioned
   demand overheating ... Both are areas of concern in the recent data."
  Q&A: "the parts of inflation that are harder to rationalize as being
   from temporary supply shocks, namely services inflation and all of the
   AI data center buildup adjacent driven inflation. Those in my mind
   correlate more strongly with overheating demand."
  → Services persistence (held since May 12) is unchanged as the core
    concern; AI-adjacent inflation is promoted alongside it as a second
    demand marker. He still says he is "sorting that out ... there's
    conflicting evidence" on supply vs. demand — the verdict is not
    closed, but both of his named tests point toward demand.
Own language — expectations:
  "longer-run market-based inflation expectations have remained
   remarkably stable ... [but] if you look at survey-based expectations
   measures and especially ... two-year, one to two-year which are
   relevant for wage and price setting there's been a lot of movement on
   those and we should not ignore [them]." [Q&A]
  → New: a short-horizon survey-expectations caveat. Not in July 14.
Duration framing, with a correction:
  "I don't agree with the characterization that we've basically been
   above the target for five and a half years, making no progress ...
   in the last year, year and a half [we] stopped making progress, first
   stalled out and then at several points it's going the wrong way."
Shift vs. prior speech (Jul 14):  HAWKISH — the tariff one-and-done view
  is withdrawn; persistence is now the presumption; the multi-month bar
  he set in July is reported unmet.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Jul 14):      same document — HAWKISH.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
MONETARY POLICY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "raise the target range ... by 1/4 percentage point" [Sep 16 stmt]
  → Speaker version: "we know what has to happen in that circumstance";
    "that's why I supported this decision" [Q&A]. A non-voter endorsing
    the hike in the first person — the Chicago seat backs the 12–0.
Own language — KEY (the policy conclusion of the framework):
  "If the forecast calls for large, persistent, recurring shocks, the
   central bank still has to restore price stability under its legal
   mandate—and the only way to bring inflation down is to raise rates and
   narrow the gap between supply and demand, even if it's not in the exact
   same sectors where the cost shocks are occurring."
  "the first answer is, don't do nothing. Looking through won't work."
  "Our policy response to persistent supply shocks may not need to be as
   large as it would be if the inflation were coming from demand
   overheating. But it won't be painless either ... the only way back is
   the hard way."
  → Two bounds in one passage: (a) the direction is up and the cost is
    accepted ("won't be painless"); (b) the magnitude is smaller than a
    demand-shock response would be, because sticky wages make the
    supply-shock trade-off real. He is arguing for a hike, not a cycle —
    unless the demand tests (services, AI) fire, in which case "there is
    no ambiguity."
Own language — the retained cut path (conditional, unchanged in form):
  "I'm among the more optimistic folks that we can get back on path
   inflation headed to 2% and if ... we have some convincing evidence
   that we're headed back to 2%, I have no problem with rates going back,
   you know, heading back down to where I think they're going to." [Q&A]
  "I'm open to being shown that the inflation problem we have is not a
   persistent problem, but is coming from a supply shock that is going to
   go away. But I want to see the evidence that it's going away.
   Otherwise, we know what has to happen." [Q&A]
  "we're moving into the Missouri show-me state ... We need to see some
   evidence that we're getting back on a path to 2% or else, as I say, we
   know what has to happen."
  → The July 14 conditional normalization path survives in form
    ("convincing evidence" → rates "heading back down") but the default
    has flipped: in July the default was patience with a cut path
    attached; now the default is "we know what has to happen" (more
    tightening) with a cut path attached to evidence that has not
    arrived. Same conditional, opposite base case.
  → SEP disclosure: "I've looked at the dot plot ... I'm among the more
    optimistic folks." [Inference: places his dots in the lower part of
    the September distribution — plausibly among the four 2027 dots at
    3.625 or below. Not stated.]
Own language — stance and neutral (asked directly, twice):
  "what is the monetary stance? Now we're back to the question of well
   what is r-star ... Am I taller or am I shorter than my imaginary
   friend or Sasquatch? I don't know. I don't know."
  "I have historically been loosely on the lower end that I kind of
   thought, hey, a 3% rate with 2% inflation and 1% real felt
   approximately right to me ... If you think that the productivity
   growth rate is going to be higher in the medium term because of AI
   ... that raises the long run rate. It doesn't lower it."
  → Asked to react to Warsh's "removed a dose of accommodation," he
    declines the label question entirely and answers on outcomes
    ("we do not have an employment problem and we do have an inflation
    problem"). Neither "restrictive" (the Just Voters' pre-hike label)
    nor "accommodative" (the Chair's). The r-star-as-Sasquatch line is
    his standing device (Feb 24 "not obvious ... even restrictive"), now
    with an explicit low-end prior of 3% nominal that he concedes AI
    would push UP.
On the December 2025 dissent (volunteered):
  "I kept expressing unease with the idea that we're going to front-load
   the rate cuts and just count on the inflation going away and being
   temporary because it's a supply shock. And so I ended up dissenting at
   the last of the meetings because we literally the government shut down
   and we didn't even have the data."
  → Retroactively frames his December dissent as the same
    don't-assume-transitory argument now made in the speech.
On independence (asked repeatedly): "a very narrow form of independence
  ... independence from political interference when setting the interest
  rate"; "you can't be afraid to take a punch"; "if that makes the
  central bank unpopular, then so be it." On the Barr/SVB question and on
  Powell's presence on the Committee: declines.
Task forces: "we aren't on the task forces so I don't know."
Cut signal status:  CONDITIONAL — retained in form, demoted in default
  (see above).
Hike signal status: PRESENT — endorsed the September hike; "we know what
  has to happen" if the persistence evidence does not arrive.
Shift vs. prior speech (Jul 14):  HAWKISH — from "the sooner we can
  normalize" as the operative conditional to "don't do nothing. Looking
  through won't work" as the operative rule; the cut path is now the
  exception clause.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Jul 14):      same document — HAWKISH.

SUMMARY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Net signal:    HAWKISH LEAN
               Goolsbee delivers the intellectual case for the September
               decision the statement itself did not make: persistent
               supply shocks cannot be looked through without breaking
               the 2% commitment, so "the only way back is the hard way."
               His own July positions are reversed one by one — tariffs
               are no longer one-and-done, the labor market is no longer
               "stable without being good" but simply "not a problem,"
               and the multi-month disinflation bar he set is reported
               unmet. What keeps this at a lean rather than outright
               hawkish: he bounds the magnitude (supply-shock responses
               "may not need to be as large"), keeps a conditional path
               back down, and says twice that he is still "sorting out"
               supply versus demand. Read him as the Committee's marker
               for how far the former dovish wing has moved: a
               non-voter who dissented against a cut in December now
               endorses a hike in the first person.

Language watch:
  • "the only way back is the hard way" — personal; the closing line of
    the remarks and repeated in Q&A. Watch for pickup by Collins / Daly.
  • "persistent supply shocks ... should not simply look through" — the
    rationale for the statement's deleted clause, stated by a regional
    president. Stage: DISPERSED (Goolsbee; Warsh's breadth argument is a
    cousin, not the same). Williams's attribution test is the opposite
    pole.
  • "there's not a conflict of mandates. There's just an inflation
    problem" — echoes Warsh Sep 16 ("I don't believe that the two parts
    of our mandate ... are working at cross purposes"). Stage: EMERGING
    (Chair + one regional).
  • "time for sniffing" / "data dogs" — his idiom for not yet ruling on
    supply vs. demand; if it becomes "time for walking" the next move is
    a series.
  • "show-me state" — new idiom for the evidence bar on disinflation.
  • DROPPED: "stable without being good" (four consecutive uses ended);
    "one and done" for tariffs.

Analyst flag:  YES
  → (1) He declined BOTH stance labels — "restrictive" and
        "accommodation." If the majority's "mildly restrictive" bloc
        moves toward his outcome-based framing, the stance-label
        collision flagged on Sept 16 resolves by abandoning the label.
  → (2) Coverage gap: his Aug 28 Bloomberg Odd Lots appearance ("Austan
        Goolsbee Is Worried the Economy Is Overheating," 46 min) was
        never collected; the pre-FOMC baseline here is therefore July 14,
        not his actual last pre-blackout communication. Recover via
        auto-captions if a tighter baseline is wanted.
  → (3) Next appearances: Chicago Fed luncheon Sept 29 ("The Economy
        Ahead") and Sept 30 ("Why Consumers and Economists See Different
        Economies") — the Sept 29 title promises a full outlook.
