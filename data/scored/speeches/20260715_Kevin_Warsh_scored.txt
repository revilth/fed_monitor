GROWTH
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Prepared remarks identical to Day 1 (capex surge as "the most striking
feature"; housing lags). Senate Q&A additions:

Own language:
  "The capex surge is remarkable. This is a capex investment boom that the
  country hasn't seen in more than a generation. It is the seed corn for
  productivity and GDP improvement over the next decade." (Banks exchange)
  "This surge is different. It's happened faster. It's probably going to be
  bigger, and it's accelerating in real time."
  "I would far rather have a capital expenditure boom than have large
  corporate profits go back into share buybacks and dividends."
  → Extends Day 1's capex elevation. He offers a rare soft projection —
    "My colleagues know I'm not big for forward guidance, but I would guess
    that that trend continues" — explicitly marked as outside his usual
    no-guidance discipline.

  AI-bubble question (Warnock): "booms and busts do not help the real economy
  and they don't make the central bank's job easier … Over the course of the
  last couple of months, we've seen the market cap both of the public
  companies and of private companies under some pressure. But the overall
  indices, we do seem to see a broadening out … earnings now for the last
  several quarters more broadly are moving up. I don't want to suggest that
  that should give us any complacency, but what the Fed's looking for is
  economic strength to broaden and the inflation that we talked about earlier
  to become more narrow."
  → "Strength to broaden, inflation to become more narrow" — a compact
    two-sided formulation of what would satisfy him; the closest thing to a
    stated ease-condition in either hearing day, though attached to no policy
    commitment.

  Immigration/potential GDP (Tillis): "Potential GDP is a function of two
  things. How many hours are we working … and then second productivity. If
  at the end of the day the worker hours are constant, we need a productivity
  boom." Declined twice to characterize immigration policy itself.

Shift vs. prior (Jul 14, Day 1):  unchanged — same capex-centric growth story,
  same supply-side optimism, same refusal to convert it into a rate argument.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC:  N/A.

LABOR MARKET
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Prepared remarks identical to Day 1 ("broadly stable"; job creation kept pace;
few layoffs; solid nominal wage growth). Senate Q&A additions:

Own verdict (Cortez Masto exchange) — the cleanest dual-mandate ranking yet:
  "Overall, I would say the labor market part of our mandate looks pretty
   good. The price stability part of our mandate looks less good."
  Also: "the clear judgment of my colleagues and me … is the labor market
  broadly looks in balance."
  → Same prioritization as Day 1's "labor markets look to be in pretty good
    balance. We've got some work to do on the inflation front," now stated
    as an explicit two-mandate comparison.

Own qualifier (aggregate vs. lived experience):
  "during periods of transition, it's often true that the labor markets in
   aggregate look better than people feel in different parts and during
   disruption, they try to recreate a job they had before through the gig
   economy and other means. We take it into account."
  → Acknowledges part-time/multiple-job-holding concerns without letting them
    move the aggregate verdict.

  AI and college graduates (Warner exchange): more confident on output than
  on labor — "I am more confident that there will be a surge in output
  because the US is at the cutting edge of AI than any certainty about the
  effect on the labor market in the next few years." Long term "absolutely
  not" catastrophic job loss, "but will it be disruptive and will some people
  have their jobs at jeopardy because of the new technologies? On that, I
  can't offer any sort of guarantee or comfort."

Shift vs. prior (Jul 14, Day 1):  unchanged — "broadly stable / in balance"
  verdict intact; the two-mandate ranking (labor fine, prices "less good")
  restates Day 1's prioritization in sharper form.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC:  N/A.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
INFLATION
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Prepared remarks identical to Day 1 ("no tolerance for persistently elevated
inflation"; "inflation is a choice"; five-years framing). Senate Q&A:

Own language — KEY (response to the two benign prints):
  "In the last couple of days, we've gotten data on consumer price index,
   today on the producer price index. Any central bank would be happy to have
   the data going in the right direction. My view is these are all imperfect
   measures of the state of underlying inflation." (Scott exchange, in the
   context of the data task force)
  → A warmer acknowledgment than Day 1's "not mission accomplished" register
    — but immediately capped by "imperfect measures," which reframes two
    benign prints as incomplete evidence. The vigilance structure is
    identical to Day 1; only the surface temperature differs. He did not
    repeat "mission accomplished" phrasing in the Senate hearing.

  The duration clock (Cortez Masto): "I'm confident in telling you it's been
  above target for 63 months … But I don't want to suggest I'm happy with any
  of those measures. I'm not."
  → The "63 months" running tally carries over from Day 1, deployed here to
    deflect a partisan comparison (3% in Jan 2025 vs. ~3.5% now) into the
    duration frame.

  Permanence and ownership (Kennedy exchange):
  "It's not going to be permanent under my watch, Senator."
  Three named actions: (1) "for those people in markets that think the Fed
  really was comfortable with a higher level of inflation, we're doing our
  best to disabuse them"; (2) "We're going to take ownership of it"; (3) "We're
  going to look at our tools in the changing economy, both balance sheet and
  interest rate, and see whether we need to adjust policy to take it head-on."
  → Restates Day 1's commitment-ownership-tools formula, with the notable
    specification that the TOOLS include the balance sheet (see MONETARY
    POLICY below).

  Inflation definition (Kennedy, Cortez Masto): "Inflation is caused when a
  one-time change in prices broaden out"; asked what causes it: "Monetary
  policy, sir." What the Fed can do: "there's a lot we can do to make sure
  that the entire grocery aisle doesn't have higher prices."

  AI and inflation — the nuance (Reed exchange, responding to Waller/Williams
  having "warned that AI investments may be increasing inflation"):
  "This is one of the good family fights … The shock of AI, the supply shock
   of AI has an effect on demand and supply. We see the effect on demand much
   more quickly … I don't view a one-time change in prices as necessarily
   being inflationary because I think there's a supply response in that way.
   This is different from a foreign conflict and what it might do, which
   tends to reduce the supply side of the economy. Will it increase measured
   prices over the course of the next 12 months? I suspect it will. Whether
   that's inflationary or not, that's up to the Federal Reserve."
  → He accepts the near-term price effect of AI demand but rejects treating
    it as inflation, on supply-response grounds — explicitly distinguishing
    his view from colleagues'. [Inference: this lowers the weight he puts on
    the AI-demand driver relative to Williams and Cook, who both list it as
    a live inflation vector — a substantive intra-committee analytical split
    beneath the shared hold.]

  M2 / monetary aggregates (Hagerty exchange): monetary aggregates restored
  to the Monetary Policy Report ("an Easter egg that we hid in there") after
  ~a decade out: "a modern central banker should have a mosaic of information
  and should not be allergic to any data that happens to be inconsistent with
  dogma … I have this old-fashioned view that monetary policy has something
  to do with money … had we seen the surge and paid attention to the surge in
  inflation and money in 21-22, we might have seen this problem sooner."
  → Not monetarism ("I do not show up here as a monetarist") but a cross-check
    doctrine; consistent with the data task force and "new measures" agenda.

Shift vs. prior (Jul 14, Day 1):  unchanged in structure — vigilance intact,
  duration clock intact. Tone on the data one notch warmer ("going in the
  right direction") with the skeptic's cap ("imperfect measures") doing the
  work "not mission accomplished" did on Day 1.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC:  N/A.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
MONETARY POLICY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Own language — rate path:
  No rate signal anywhere in ~2 hours of Senate Q&A, matching Day 1's
  three-hour demonstration of the no-forward-guidance policy. Pressed by
  Kennedy for options: "Leave rates alone … That is an option, Senator." /
  Raise: "Another option." / Cut: "A third option, a non-exclusive list of
  options, but yes." — all three directions kept deliberately open 13 days
  before the July 28–29 meeting.

  On Waller's July 13 hike conditionality (Tillis raised it directly):
  "There are diversity of views. I wouldn't want to dwell on any one of them
   in particular."
  → Second consecutive day declining to engage Waller's framework — neither
    endorsement nor rejection. The orchestrator's silence continues.

  Consensus framing (Tillis): asked if he has "a magic wand" to move the
  FOMC: "perhaps I wish I do but I do not have such a wand," agreeing the
  Fed is "a consensus-led organization."

BALANCE SHEET — the doctrine extended (Ricketts exchange):
  "Monetary policy is made currently through at least two material
   instruments, interest rates and the balance sheet."
  "As we think about inflation risks and we think about the conduct of
   monetary policy at large, we have two instruments that we should be
   thinking about. The balance sheet is one of them."
  → Day 1 established balance sheet ≠ plumbing; Day 2 goes further by naming
    the balance sheet as an instrument in the context of INFLATION RISKS —
    the first time he has connected the balance sheet to the current
    inflation fight rather than to regime design in the abstract. [Inference:
    this puts balance-sheet runoff/composition on the table as a potential
    tightening margin, distinct from a rate hike. Not a near-term signal —
    he immediately routes it through the task force — but directionally
    hawkish for the balance-sheet debate.]
  Preferred end-state: "interest rate policy should be the driver of monetary
  policy. The balance sheet should be as small as practicable to conduct
  operations," expanding in crisis or for liquidity needs; "a permanently
  large balance sheet with a duration of assets … larger than the duration of
  assets of treasuries … that sure seems a little bit more like fiscal policy
  than I'm comfortable with. So, I would like us to have a leaner, meaner
  balance sheet."
  Process: "monetary policy needs to be architected … by the policymakers
  around the FOMC. The plumbing, the operations … is conducted by the New
  York Fed. I want the architecture to drive the plumbing, not the plumbing
  to drive the architecture." Changes would be "well discussed, well shared
  and … quite deliberate," with markets given "plenty of time to adjust."

Fed communications doctrine (Blunt Rochester exchange):
  "There are plenty of people on Wall Street who are upset with me already
   that I'm somehow not feeding them all the information they'd gotten before
   and if they only had my dot, everything would be swell. My message to them
   is: play the ball, don't play the Fed. By that, I mean figure out what's
   happening in the real economy. Respond to data that's happening in the
   real economy."
  → The most quotable formulation yet of the guidance-withdrawal regime — a
    direct instruction to markets to stop trading Fed communication. Note the
    "my dot" aside: consistent with his non-submission to the June SEP.

Task force timeline (Cortez Masto): six months, not years — briefings to the
  FOMC "as early as September," conclusions "by year end," details and "much
  of the deliberations … in the public square."

Independence (Van Hollen, Kennedy, Alsobrooks exchanges):
  "I don't want to be in the business of sharing discussions that the
   president and I have." (declining to confirm or deny communications)
  "They chose an independent guy to do an independent job, and that's exactly
   what I plan on doing."
  "The president has not … tried to influence the conduct of monetary policy.
   And if he tried to, I would continue to keep my head down and do the job."
  "The president never asked me to do anything inappropriate and if he did,
   I wouldn't do it."
  Calendar disclosure: "full compliance with the law" / ongoing FOIA — no
  commitment to Powell-style voluntary disclosure of presidential contacts.
  → Substantively consistent with Day 1's "sacrosanct" formulations, but the
    refusal to characterize presidential conversations is a step less
    forthcoming than the Day 1 register; he substitutes assurances about
    conduct for disclosure of contact. Ranking-member and Warren/Reed/
    Alsobrooks exchanges on his asset sales and the Bowman blackout-dinner IG
    referral were contentious but carry no policy signal.

Cut signal status: ABSENT (consistent across all post-June-17 appearances).
Hike conditionality: ABSENT — Waller's framework again left unengaged.

Shift vs. prior (Jul 14, Day 1):  UNCHANGED on rates — no-signal posture
  held through a second full hearing. The balance-sheet-as-inflation-tool
  formulation and "play the ball, don't play the Fed" are the genuinely new
  doctrine statements; independence language shifts from declarative
  ("sacrosanct") to non-disclosure of presidential conversations.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC:  N/A.

SUMMARY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Net signal:    NEUTRAL by design, vigilance intact — consistent with Day 1.

  Warsh completed two consecutive days of congressional testimony (~5 hours
  of Q&A) without a single rate-path signal, keeping all three options
  explicitly open 13 days before the July meeting. His treatment of the
  benign CPI/PPI pair is the tell: "any central bank would be happy to have
  the data going in the right direction" concedes the direction, "imperfect
  measures" withholds the conclusion — the same vigilance structure as
  Day 1's "not mission accomplished," one notch warmer in tone.

  Three genuinely new pieces of intelligence from the Senate round:
  (1) The balance sheet is now explicitly framed as one of "two instruments"
      relevant to INFLATION RISKS — extending the Day 1 doctrine from regime
      design toward the active policy toolkit, with "leaner, meaner balance
      sheet" as the stated preference.
  (2) He publicly split with Waller and Williams on AI-investment inflation:
      a one-time price change with a coming supply response is "not
      necessarily inflationary" — he discounts the committee's newest
      inflation driver even while pledging vigilance on inflation broadly.
  (3) "Play the ball, don't play the Fed" — the communication regime
      distilled to a market instruction, plus a hard timeline for the task
      forces (September briefings, year-end conclusions).

  He again declined to engage Waller's hike conditionality by name —
  two hearings, two opportunities, two deliberate passes.

Language watch:
  • "imperfect measures" (of underlying inflation) — the standing device for
    discounting favorable prints; second consecutive day. Stage: DISPERSED
    (Warsh only).
  • "two instruments" / balance sheet against "inflation risks" — watch
    whether Logan (Jul 16, the committee's balance-sheet specialist) engages
    it. Stage: DISPERSED.
  • "Play the ball, don't play the Fed" — likely media pickup; watch whether
    it becomes the shorthand for the no-guidance regime. Stage: DISPERSED.
  • "63 months" duration clock — second consecutive day. Stage: DISPERSED
    (Warsh only).
  • "Leaner, meaner balance sheet" — quotable; pairs with Day 1's "price
    taker, not price maker." Stage: DISPERSED.

Analyst flag:  YES
  → (1) The balance-sheet escalation: naming the balance sheet as an
    inflation-relevant instrument goes beyond Day 1's regime-design framing.
    If the task force lands by year-end with runoff/duration recommendations,
    that is a tightening margin markets are not pricing. Does Logan engage
    "two instruments" on July 16?
  → (2) The AI-inflation split (Warsh vs. Waller/Williams/Cook) is now on the
    record in his own words. If AI-driven goods/electricity prices keep
    rising into September, the committee's hawks and the Chair are reading
    the same driver differently — a potential fault line in the September
    SEP and statement language.
  → (3) Tone drift check for the blackout: Day 1 "not mission accomplished"
    → Day 2 "happy to have the data going in the right direction" (capped).
    If the July 28–29 statement or presser concedes directional progress,
    the Senate phrasing was the leading indicator.
