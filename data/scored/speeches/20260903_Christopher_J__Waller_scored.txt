GROWTH
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Economic activity is expanding at a solid pace despite elevated uncertainty
   ... Productivity growth and capital investment are strong." [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Waller: "Despite the uncertainties I mentioned, most of which weigh on
    economic activity, growth in real gross domestic product (GDP) is
    continuing at a solid pace." "business investment has been strong
    throughout."

Own language:
  "Real GDP grew at a 1.8 percent annual rate in the first half of this year."
  "real private domestic final purchases, the measure of spending that best
   reflects the underlying strength of consumer demand, rose a strong 3 percent
   in the first half of 2026."
  "I expect real GDP to grow a bit more than 2 percent this year, a respectable
   outcome considering the uncertainties I mentioned."
  July retail sales: "weak. But this decrease reflected the fact that Amazon's
   Prime Day promotion was held in June, dragging down seasonally adjusted
   sales in July. Outside of 'non-store' sales, retail spending grew."
  Wealth effect: "Given the rise in wealth from the increase in equity prices
   this year, I believe this should sustain consumption growth."
  AI investment — refuses the compositional discount: "Some would argue that
   this investment in a narrow sector that tends to be capital intensive,
   rather than labor intensive, is misleadingly propping up GDP and should in
   some sense be discounted. I don't agree. AI investment is a legitimate part
   of GDP today."
  → The compositional concern he rejects is the one Cook and Daly have raised;
    he takes a side.

Shift vs. prior speech (Jul 13):  UNCHANGED — July 13: "Economic activity
  continues to be solid," PDFP "likely rose strongly," "the real side of the
  economy is in good shape." Same read, now with a full-year forecast and the
  AI-not-discounted argument attached.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Jul 13):      Same comparison — unchanged.

LABOR MARKET
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Job gains have kept pace with the workforce, and the unemployment rate has
   changed little." [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Waller: "Job creation, though a bit volatile, has increased this year by
    an average of 60,000 a month through July. That is close to and probably a
    bit above estimates of what it takes to keep pace with the slow growth in
    the labor force—mostly because of much lower net immigration."
  → The statement's "kept pace" is rendered with the mechanism (low labor
    supply growth from lower immigration).

Own verdict:
  "the labor market, it is also in satisfactory shape."
  "While there were indications in the second half of 2025 of easing labor
   demand, relative to supply, those signs evaporated."
  "the unemployment rate has fallen a bit to 4.1 percent in July, a
   historically low rate and slightly below the median of FOMC participants'
   long-run or equilibrium rate. Layoffs and initial claims for unemployment
   insurance are likewise low."
  "As of today, the labor market is stable, with employment near its maximum
   sustainable level."

Own qualifier / addition:
  "With economic activity and the labor market in good shape, they are not a
   large factor in my determination of the appropriate setting of monetary
   policy. But they are an important backdrop for the part of the outlook that
   is my focus right now, inflation."
  "I expect more of the same tomorrow when we get the August employment
   report."
  → Labor is removed from his decision. The −23K July print is absorbed into
    the "60,000 a month through July" average without being named. Third
    official in a week to supply a mechanism for reading low payroll gains as
    non-signal (Schmid Aug 27: retirements; Warsh Aug 28: "when labor supply
    is barely growing"; Waller: lower net immigration).

Shift vs. prior speech (Jul 13):  UNCHANGED — July 13: "employment is close
  to its maximum sustainable level, neither a source of concern for the
  strength of the six-year economic expansion nor a source of inflationary
  pressure"; "Unless I see evidence of a significantly weakening labor market,
  my focus will be on inflation." Same verdict and same gatekeeper. New
  detail: unemployment "slightly below" the long-run median; "those signs
  evaporated" on 2025's easing.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Jul 13):      Same comparison — unchanged.

INFLATION
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Inflation remains elevated relative to the Committee's 2 percent goal, in
   part reflecting supply shocks that have driven price increases in certain
   sectors, including energy." [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Waller: "Inflation is elevated significantly above the FOMC's 2 percent
    goal and has exceeded that target for five and a half years."
  → On the supply shocks he concedes the level and denies the ongoing force:
    "I don't see elevated energy prices and tariffs now as a significant
    source of ongoing inflation pressure. The evidence is that the price
    effects of tariffs have largely passed through inflation, and my earlier
    worry that higher energy prices would bleed into many goods and services
    prices hasn't come to pass, at least so far."
    (Williams, Sep 2, keeps the shocks as "the biggest drivers"; Waller says
    they are spent. Both readings are dovish in effect.)

Own language — KEY:
  "while inflation remains meaningfully above the Federal Open Market
   Committee's (FOMC) 2 percent goal, recent data suggest we are finally seeing
   some signs of disinflation."
  Levels: "PCE prices are up 3.7 percent in the past 12 months, and core PCE
   inflation is 3.3 percent ... they are not the best guide for where
   inflation is today."
  The measure he uses: "Three-month core inflation is 3.05 percent for the
   three months through July, a level that is still not consistent with the
   FOMC's 2 percent goal. Nevertheless, it is important to note the trend.
   Three-month inflation has fallen steadily from 4.76 percent in February.
   That is a considerable improvement, and the speed of this downward
   trajectory is encouraging."
  → A momentum test. Warsh's Aug 28 standard asks whether underlying inflation
    is moving to target "clearly and at sufficient speed" and answers no on a
    breadth measure; Waller answers the speed question yes on a three-month
    core measure. Same question, different measure, opposite verdict.

Own language — the nonmarket-services adjustment (new):
  "what caught my eye in the last PCE report is that nonmarket services prices
   accounted for approximately half of the increase in core prices. As you are
   probably aware, I don't like throwing out specific categories going into
   the estimate of PCE inflation, but nonmarket services prices have always
   been an issue for me, since they are imputed and not actual price changes.
   So, ignoring this one factor, my take is that underlying inflation is doing
   better than the core numbers suggest."
  "a pending change in the way the Commerce Department estimates the fees paid
   to stock market traders and related professionals ... could lower 12-month
   PCE inflation by a few tenths of a percentage point ... this is a welcome
   measurement correction."
  → A statistical revision, not an economic change, that he expects "shortly."
    Note the contrast with Warsh, who counts all 199 components.

Upside risks — named:
  "Energy prices have moved up again and remain significantly higher than they
   were at the beginning of 2026, and the economy faces both pressure on
   technology goods prices related to the AI buildout and the possibility of
   more tariff increases."
  Wages: "wage growth, once one accounts for productivity growth, is broadly
   consistent with an expectation that inflation is continuing to come down to
   2 percent."
  Expectations: "we haven't seen a significant increase in longer-term
   inflation expectations, but it must be acknowledged as a risk—one that the
   FOMC should be prepared to act on if longer-term expectations rise and
   progress on inflation reverses."

Not repeated from July 13: the breadth statistic ("nearly 70 percent of
  [core services] categories have 3-month and 12-month inflation over 3
  percent") and the characterization of core as on an "upward trajectory."

Shift vs. prior speech (Jul 13):  DOVISH — July 13: core "has steadily moved
  up ... from 3 percent in December 2025 to 3.4 percent in May," with an
  "equally plausible" case that inflation "will remain at its elevated level
  or even trend higher." September 3: "signs of disinflation," three-month
  core 4.76% → 3.05%, tariff pass-through "largely" done, energy bleed
  "hasn't come to pass." The AI goods channel (Jul 13) is retained as an
  upside risk; the breadth measure is dropped.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Jul 13):      Same comparison — dovish.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
MONETARY POLICY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "The Committee decided to maintain the target range ..." [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Waller: "I was comfortable supporting the FOMC's decision in late July to
    hold rates steady because the data at that time led me to view the real
    side of the economy as solid and we finally saw hints that disinflation
    may be starting. So I was willing to be patient and see if the
    disinflation would continue."
  The statement carries no guidance; Waller supplies his own conditional.

Own language — KEY (explicit, named two-branch conditional, stated twice):
  Opening: "If this continues in the data due over the next two weeks, I would
   be inclined to support holding the target for the federal funds rate at its
   current setting ... If the incoming data for August show this improvement
   has been fleeting, then it may be appropriate to raise the policy rate when
   the FOMC meets on September 15 and 16."
  Closing: "my decision on the appropriate stance of policy will be heavily
   influenced by what we learn about August inflation. If there is continued
   progress toward our 2 percent goal, then I am willing to support holding
   the policy rate at its current level. But if inflation comes in hot, I
   would consider a rate hike."
  → Scenario framing is EXPLICIT AND NAMED, with a dated trigger (the August
    inflation data) and a named meeting. He says so himself: "I have outlined
    what it would take for me to support a continued pause as well as what
    would cause me to support tighter policy." "Hot" is not quantified.

Own language — restrictiveness and sensitivity:
  "I judge that policy is currently only slightly restricting aggregate demand,
   and it may not take much acceleration in inflation to nudge me into
   supporting tighter policy. If there is evidence that progress toward 2
   percent inflation reversed in August, a small adjustment in our stance
   would help ensure that it resumes."
  → First stance characterization since May 22 ("still at a restrictive
    setting"; July 13 had none). "Only slightly restricting" sits with the
    "mildly restrictive" group (Paulson, Collins, Daly; Cook in Q&A) rather
    than with Warsh's "hard pressed to describe ... as restrictive" or the
    dissenters' "not appropriately restrictive." He draws a different
    operational conclusion from it than that group: because the margin is
    thin, a small inflation surprise moves him. "A small adjustment" — one
    move, not a series.

Own language — policy communication (the second half of the speech):
  Three types: (1) explaining the current position; (2) the reaction function
  — "IF the data comes in a particular way, THEN I will advocate for policy to
  be set a particular way ... it is not a commitment to a policy action—it is
  a conditional policy statement"; (3) forward guidance — "a path for the
  policy rate that is essentially independent of incoming data ... most
  warranted when the policy rate is at the effective lower bound."
  "I agree with Chairman Warsh that forward guidance isn't appropriate now or
   in many other situations. But when it is truly needed, I believe it should
   be used."
  "Now, is that reaction function perfect? No ... I do not let perfection
   become the enemy of the good."
  "I view myself as a home plate umpire in baseball ... Both want to play the
   ball, but they cannot do that until they know the umpire's strike zone. The
   strike zone is the umpire's reaction function."
  → Against Warsh Aug 28: Warsh rejected forward guidance AND an explicit
    reaction function ("our knowledge just doesn't extend that far ... works
    better in theory than in practice"). Waller accepts the first and defends
    the second, using Warsh's own idiom ("play the ball, not the referee,"
    Jul 29; "play the ball, don't play the Fed," Jul 15) to argue that
    playing the ball requires knowing the referee's rule. On forward guidance
    itself his position is unchanged from July 6 ("in some cases, it's best
    not to use it at all"); the explicit alignment with the Chair is new.
  Historical example: September 2021 guidance moved the two-year yield 200bp
  by March 2022 before any rate move — "forward guidance helped tighten
  economic conditions long before we actually raised the policy rate."

Cut signal status:  ABSENT (third consecutive outlook speech).
Hike signal status: PRESENT as a conditional branch; no longer the near-term
  consideration it was on July 13. Trigger: the August inflation print.

Shift vs. prior speech (Jul 13):  DOVISH on the base case — July 13's hold
  branch required "several months of lower readings"; the hike branch was
  "If we get another hot reading on core inflation this week, then the FOMC
  will need to consider tightening monetary policy in the near term." The
  readings since were not hot, and hold is now his stated inclination.
  UNCHANGED-TO-TIGHTER on trigger sensitivity ("it may not take much"). New:
  the "only slightly restricting" characterization. Not repeated: July 13's
  "avoid overtightening ... and risking a recession" / "fighting the last
  war" guardrails — replaced by sizing ("a small adjustment").
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Jul 13):      Same comparison.

SUMMARY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Net signal:    DOVISH LEAN (vs. July 13), with a live, dated hike branch
               The Committee's most explicit hike conditional has resolved
               to a hold base case: the July 13 trigger (a hot core reading)
               did not fire, and Waller now reports "signs of disinflation,"
               three-month core falling from 4.76% to 3.05%, tariff effects
               "largely passed through," and imputed nonmarket services
               flattering core. Growth and labor are unchanged and explicitly
               excluded from his decision. What keeps the read from being
               plainly dovish: "only slightly restricting aggregate demand,"
               "it may not take much acceleration in inflation," and a named
               hike branch for September 15–16 keyed to the August print.

               Position relative to the Committee: on the inflation trend he
               is with Williams (Sep 2), by a momentum test rather than an
               attribution test, and against Warsh's breadth verdict. On the
               stance he is in the "mildly restrictive" group, not the
               not-restrictive group. On labor he is with Warsh and Schmid
               in pre-discounting weak payrolls. On AI he rejects the
               compositional discount Cook and Daly apply. On communication
               he is the first official to align with the Chair on forward
               guidance while defending reaction functions against him.

Language watch:
  • "only slightly restricting aggregate demand" — stance characterization
    from a voting Governor. Stage: COMMITTEE-WIDE among the hold majority
    ("mildly restrictive": Paulson, Collins, Daly; Cook Q&A); the Chair and
    the dissenters are outside it.
  • "it may not take much acceleration in inflation to nudge me into
    supporting tighter policy" — thin-margin trigger. Stage: DISPERSED (first
    use).
  • Three-month core 4.76% → 3.05%, "the speed of this downward trajectory is
    encouraging" — the momentum answer to Warsh's "at sufficient speed."
    Stage: DISPERSED. A second official citing the three-month series would
    put the momentum measure in open competition with breadth.
  • Nonmarket / imputed services exclusion and the pending Commerce revision
    ("a few tenths") — Stage: DISPERSED (first use). A checkable, near-dated
    item.
  • "The strike zone is the umpire's reaction function" / "I do not let
    perfection become the enemy of the good" — Stage: DISPERSED (first use);
    the counter-idiom to Warsh's "play the ball, not the referee."
  • "signs of disinflation" — Stage: EMERGING (Williams Sep 2 "trend ...
    moving slowly down"; minutes "most participants anticipated that
    inflation would step down").

Analyst flag:  YES
  → (1) One voter's September position is now a published function of the
    August inflation print: continued progress → hold; "hot" → he would
    consider a hike. "Hot" is not numbered; with three-month core at 3.05%
    and falling, the bar for "fleeting" looks high, but it is his bar.
  → (2) Three definitions of "underlying inflation" in six days — Warsh
    (breadth), Williams (attribution), Waller (momentum) — with opposite
    verdicts. Definitional, not empirical; watch which one the September
    statement and SEP discussion adopt.
  → (3) The Commerce measurement change: a revision to nonmarket services
    that could cut 12-month PCE by "a few tenths," expected "shortly." If it
    lands around the meeting it improves the hold case with no change in
    conditions. Verify the timing.
  → (4) The Prime Day explanation for weak July retail sales is checkable
    against the seasonal adjustment; if right, one of the few soft demand
    prints in the cycle disappears.
  → (5) The Reuters Q&A is not published. Given the prepared text carries
    both the conditional and the disagreement with the Chair, recover a
    transcript if one appears.
