THE STRUCTURE — A NAMED, TWO-SIDED SCENARIO FRAMEWORK
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
This is the most analytically explicit document of the post-FOMC cycle. She
lays out three overlapping shocks, then two named scenarios with different
policy responses attached:

  SCENARIO ONE (conventional dynamics): "inflation temporarily rises and then
   dissipates as the shocks subside. But the current mildly restrictive policy
   that's in place in the US is sufficient to gradually return inflation to the
   2% target. It would just push it out a little bit."
   → Policy response: "you might fine-tune the policy level, but you don't
     really need a large change."

  SCENARIO TWO (compounding): "these overlapping shocks actually have this
   compounding effect … the effects on inflation are amplified because they've
   happened at the same time and they're occurring at a point in time when
   inflation's been above target for over five years. That would suggest that
   inflation has somehow broadened and it's become more persistent."
   → Policy response: "policy must be recalibrated and not a little bit but
     probably more substantially … it would be insufficient to then just do
     small calibrations, almost surgical calibrations on policy."

THE KEY SENTENCE — the probability weighting has moved:
  "I increasingly … if you ask me, I think scenario one is still my modal, but
   if you do the percentages of the modal versus the scenario 2, I think they're
   much closer together than they used to be. And so it's really important that
   we not just push scenario two off as a risk that goes in alternative scenario
   thinking, but that it becomes something we really consider."
  → She keeps the benign case as modal but explicitly narrows the gap and
    promotes the adverse case out of the tail into active deliberation. Her CEO
    metaphor makes it concrete: "both of these scenarios are on the middle of
    the desk … getting equal attention."
  → This is the hawkish move of the speech, and it is a probability shift, not
    a verdict change.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
GROWTH
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Growth is not treated as a mandate concern; it appears only through the shock
decomposition. The AI investment shock is the one she cannot yet call:

  "The real news if you will right now is technology related spending and price
   inflation … If you look at technology equipment PCE inflation, it's rising
   sharply this year and is contributing an outsized share to overall inflation
   … That is a dynamic where I can't show you the positive picture of it's
   peaked and it's coming down. This is just something that's continuing to go
   up and it remains an open question whether this is a relative demand shock
   and just changes the relative price of technology equipment … or if it's
   something broader than that because it's competing for resources in a way
   that spills over into overall inflation."
  → The most careful treatment of AI capex on the committee: she frames the
    relative-price vs. general-inflation question explicitly and REFUSES to
    resolve it. Contrast Cook (Aug 5), who resolves it dovishly (pressure "will
    ease"), and Paulson (Aug 4), who files it as a reason policy may not be
    restrictive enough.

Resource-crowding — a genuinely new mechanism in the corpus:
  "that look can't be simply what do the AI firms think they're creating but in
   fact what do the other businesses feel like they're not getting or they're
   paying more for because the AI investment boom is taking resources that they
   would otherwise have access [to], creating a scarcity problem that we haven't
   seen yet to my knowledge in the western United States … but it is something
   we need to watch."

Direction: neutral — growth is an input to the shock analysis, not a concern.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
LABOR MARKET
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Own verdict — labor exculpated from inflation, with a productivity mechanism:
  "right now, we just don't see much pressure, cost push pressure from the labor
   market in terms of price inflation … [wages are] not growing beyond 2%
   inflation and productivity. And in fact, if you plot productivity growth, you
   start to see a gap emerge between the productivity that's being measured in
   the economy and the pay that workers are receiving, just suggesting that
   firms are able to handle some of the cost pressure through gains in
   productivity as opposed to … raising prices for consumers."

Contact intelligence — margin defence through cost-cutting:
  "We hear a lot of this when we talk to CEOs … they really are working to
   change the cost profile of their firm. Some of that is being enabled by AI,
   but some of it's just being enabled by just another look at business
   processes, trying to do more with fewer workers."
  → Note the implication she does not draw: firms doing "more with fewer
    workers" is disinflationary now and a labor-demand risk later. She files it
    under the disinflation case only.

She names a labor-side trip wire for scenario two: firms telling her "they
either have to pay more to their workers because of the burden of inflation …
or their margins are compressed so much they're going to try to pass input cost
along."

Direction: neutral / dovish on inflation pressure — same conclusion as Paulson
  ("you're not seeing pressures on inflation in the labor market"), reached
  through a productivity mechanism she supplies and Paulson does not.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
INFLATION
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Inflation remains elevated … in part reflecting supply shocks" [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Daly's entire keynote is an interrogation of whether that clause justifies
    the look-through it implies. She neither defends it flatly (Paulson) nor
    qualifies its composition (Cook) — she asks whether the DOCTRINE behind it
    still holds when shocks overlap.

The conventional wisdom, stated precisely and then put at risk:
  "if the shock is temporary, the right appropriate monetary policy is to look
   through it. The reason being … monetary policy acts with a lag, 12 to 18
   months … by the time you've raised the rate and that's worked its way
   through, you haven't affected the shock dynamic, but you've definitely
   affected the economy and unnecessarily so."
  → This is EXACTLY Paulson's timing argument (Aug 4: "I would have been too
    late"), stated as textbook doctrine. Daly then does what Paulson does not:
    she asks whether it survives three overlapping shocks.

THE COMPOUNDING QUESTION — her original contribution:
  "before the tariff shock is fully through the economy, the oil shock comes …
   And then before that's fully dissipated, the AI shock comes … are they going
   to have a compounding effect? … Will that serve to compound the effects,
   interact those effects and make this either broader or more persistent …
   greater than the sum of its parts."
  → Structurally the same worry as Kashkari's "series of successive supply
    shocks," but derived analytically with a forecast decomposition rather than
    by historical analogy, and — critically — left OPEN rather than resolved.
    Kashkari concludes look-through has failed; Daly says we do not yet know.

FOUR NAMED REASONS FOR SCENARIO ONE (the benign case):
  1. Limited pricing power — "businesses in the United States have very limited
     pricing power. They just don't have the ability to pass fully the cost that
     they're facing on to consumers," evidenced by NY Fed (services) and
     Richmond Fed (manufacturing) surveys showing expected costs above expected
     selling prices; and consumers "trading down, moving on."
     → Direct corroboration of Paulson's Aug 4 pricing-power ceiling, from
       survey data rather than CEO conversations. Two officials, independent
       evidence, same mechanism.
  2. Inflation expectations track gasoline, not broad inflation — unlike the
     post-pandemic episode where expectations ran far above energy: "gasoline
     prices are driving the lion's share … of the change in inflation
     expectations."
  3. Labor market not generating cost-push (above).
  4. Long-run expectations "remarkably well anchored" — but see the bounding
     below.

TWO NAMED REASONS FOR SCENARIO TWO:
  1. The attention threshold — "3.9% is the average inflation rate between 2021
     and 2026 … a forthcoming AER paper … argues that consumers' attention to
     inflation … impacts inflation itself … this author actually estimates that
     the threshold where people's attention focuses on inflation is 4%. And
     we're very close to 4%. We are 3.9 on average and 3.7 of late."
     → A quantified, near-breached trip wire. New to the corpus and the single
       most concrete escalation trigger any official has named this cycle.
  2. The producer-consumer price gap — "producer prices are just outpacing
     consumer prices … PPI in June is 5.5 and consumer price index grows by 3.5
     … if you index these to 21, the cumulative change still leaves firms
     behind … at some point firms want to maintain their margins."
     Plus a contagion mechanism: "if the firm across the street from me is
     heavily energy dependent and raising prices and the firm down the street
     is heavily affected by tariffs and raising prices, I might just get into
     the ethos of everybody else is raising prices. Why don't I try to? That
     hasn't happened yet to our ability to discern it. But I think it's not a
     risk we can just push aside and say because we haven't seen it, it isn't
     there."
     → NOTE THE TENSION SHE IS TRACKING: reason 1 for scenario one (limited
       pricing power) and reason 2 for scenario two (compressed margins forcing
       pass-through) are the SAME FACT read on two horizons. Her framework is
       internally consistent because pricing power is a stock that depletes.
       This is the sharpest thing in the speech and it is what Paulson's Aug 4
       version lacks.

ANCHORED EXPECTATIONS — comfort explicitly refused (Q&A, KEY):
  Asked whether the Fed can wait until expectations show signs of unanchoring:
  → "Absolutely not. By the time the anchor drifts, it's really hard to get it
    back … I think that same vigor has to be applied when we think it could go
    up the other way. The reason I showed it was just to say we don't have a
    current alarm bell sounding off."
  → And: "the longer inflation expectations remain well anchored through big
    shocks in inflation, the less comfortable they are in a way for evidence …
    it's small comfort if you're thinking about how long you can tolerate
    above-target inflation."
  → Converts the standard reassurance leg into an argument against waiting —
    the same inversion Cook performed on July 15 ("anchored expectations comfort
    me only to the extent…"), reached independently.
  → Also: "Congress didn't say keep inflation expectations well anchored … It
    said keep price stability in place."

Direction: HAWKISH LEAN — the level verdict is unchanged, but scenario two is
  promoted from tail to co-equal, a quantified 4% attention threshold is named
  with current readings just below it, and the anchored-expectations comfort is
  explicitly refused.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
MONETARY POLICY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
July 29 hold endorsed without reservation:
  "I was completely supportive of the decision to hold rates in July."

Stance characterization — "mildly restrictive," inside scenario one:
  "the current mildly restrictive policy that's in place in the US is sufficient
   to gradually return inflation to the 2% target."
  → Same label as Paulson (Aug 4), Collins (May 13) and Cook's July 15 Q&A. But
    note the difference in status: for Paulson it is an assessment of where
    policy IS; for Daly it is a premise that holds only in scenario one. In
    scenario two the same stance is, in her words, "out of position."

The case for waiting — information, not comfort:
  "we have another meeting coming up in September, which is now just 7 weeks
   away. And importantly, we have a lot of information we need to collect if
   we're deciding between scenario one and scenario two. If it was just
   fine-tuning that I thought we were up against, that would be a different
   problem. This is a problem where you've got one type of thing that gives you
   the patience or the ability to hold on and hold for longer and you have
   another one that says we're out of position and we need to adjust."
  → The hold is justified as scenario discrimination, not as confidence. That
    is a materially different rationale from Paulson's ("not a close call") and
    from Cook's ("three disinflation channels are in train").

Named data triggers for scenario two (Q&A, asked what would trigger a hike):
  • Tariff goods-price improvement reversing — "if that data that looked
    positive in the June release goes back up … either the new tariff
    announcements, the extension of the tariff rates … or it was just a blip …
    then I think that would be one not great sign."
  • Whether the war stabilizes and energy prices fall.
  • The AI resource-crowding channel showing up in non-AI firms' costs.

Closing commitment — the explicit pre-commitment:
  "be vigilant to watch the information as it comes in, but be very prepared to
   take the action if the second scenario in particular emerges."

Cut signal status:  ABSENT — cuts are not mentioned in any scenario.
Hike signal status: PRESENT, conditional on scenario two, and UNUSUALLY LARGE —
  she pre-commits not merely to hiking but to a NON-INCREMENTAL response
  ("not a little bit but probably more substantially"; small "surgical
  calibrations" would be "insufficient").

Direction: HAWKISH LEAN — she endorses the hold while pre-committing to a
  larger-than-incremental response in the adverse case, and moves that case out
  of the tail.

SUMMARY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Net signal:    HAWKISH LEAN, from the most analytically explicit document of the
               cycle. Daly endorses the July hold and keeps the benign case as
               her modal, so nothing in her verdict has changed. What changed is
               the weighting and the pre-commitment: scenario two is "much
               closer" to scenario one than it used to be, both are "on the
               middle of the desk," and her stated response to scenario two is
               not a hike but a substantial recalibration — explicitly ruling
               out "surgical" adjustments as insufficient.

               Two things make this the most useful document of the week
               despite coming from a non-voter. First, the compounding
               hypothesis is Kashkari's successive-shocks argument rebuilt
               analytically instead of by historical analogy — and left open
               rather than asserted, which makes it testable. Second, she names
               a quantified trip wire nobody else has: a 4% attention threshold
               against current readings of 3.9% average and 3.7% latest. That
               converts a doctrinal dispute into a measurable one.

               Her framework also resolves an apparent contradiction running
               through the majority's case. Paulson (Aug 4) cites limited
               pricing power as evidence policy is restrictive; Daly cites the
               same fact, then observes that compressed margins are precisely
               what eventually forces pass-through. Pricing power is a stock
               that depletes. That is the strongest analytical objection to the
               majority's position, and it comes from someone who voted with
               them in spirit and endorsed the hold.

Position relative to committee:
  Daly sits between the majority and the dissenters, and is the only official
  to have built a framework in which both are right under different states of
  the world. She shares Paulson's timing doctrine and pricing-power evidence,
  shares Kashkari's overlapping-shocks worry and Cook's refusal to take comfort
  from anchored expectations, and commits to a response larger than anything
  the dissenters have asked for — conditional on evidence none of them yet
  claims to have.

Language watch:
  • "compounding effect" / "greater than the sum of its parts" (overlapping
    shocks) — the analytical form of Kashkari's "series of successive supply
    shocks," and the version most likely to be adoptable by majority members
    because it is stated as an open question rather than a verdict. Watch for it
    in the July 28–29 minutes and from Jefferson or Williams. Stage: EMERGING.
  • The 4% attention threshold — a named, quantified escalation trigger with
    current readings just below it. Nothing else on the committee is this
    falsifiable except Paulson's 2.4–2.8 underlying estimate. Stage: DISPERSED
    (first use).
  • "mildly restrictive" — now Paulson, Collins, Cook (Q&A) and Daly. Stage:
    COMMITTEE-WIDE among non-Troika, and directly contested by Kashkari.
  • "policy must be recalibrated and not a little bit" — the only
    non-incremental response on record; contrast Kashkari's "small steps."
    Stage: DISPERSED.
  • "By the time the anchor drifts, it's really hard to get it back" — the
    anti-complacency inversion of anchored expectations, matching Cook's
    July 15 bounding. Stage: EMERGING (two officials, independently).

Analyst flag:  YES
  → (1) PRIMARY — the pricing-power stock/flow argument. Paulson and Daly cite
    the same survey evidence to opposite ends: Paulson as proof of
    restrictiveness, Daly as a depleting buffer whose exhaustion IS scenario
    two. Whether pricing power is a level or a countdown is the single most
    decision-relevant open question in the cycle. Watch Barkin's contact
    intelligence (Richmond manufacturers) for the earliest read.
  → (2) The 4% attention threshold vs. Paulson's 2.4–2.8% underlying estimate:
    the committee now has two quantified trip wires that point in opposite
    directions, because they measure different things (headline experience vs.
    shock-stripped core). Both are trackable. Ask which one the September
    decision is actually keyed to.
  → (3) Daly commits to a LARGER response than any dissenter requests, but only
    in scenario two. If the compounding evidence arrives, the committee's
    reaction function is more aggressive than the 9–3 vote implies — the hawkish
    tail is fatter than the vote count suggests.
  → (4) She refuses to resolve the AI relative-price-vs-general-inflation
    question that Cook resolved dovishly and Paulson resolved hawkishly, three
    days apart. Note that Daly is the one with the SF district's direct line to
    the AI firms. Her agnosticism is the most informed position on record.
  → (5) Non-voter status and her explicit disclaimer ("doesn't reflect the
    FOMC's views") bound how far to read this into committee positioning.
