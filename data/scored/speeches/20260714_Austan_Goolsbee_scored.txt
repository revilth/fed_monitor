productivity-OVERHEATING frame was not repeated in this venue.

LABOR MARKET
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Job gains have kept pace with the workforce, and the unemployment rate
   has changed little." [Jun 17 stmt]
  → Speaker version: does not echo the statement formulation; runs his
    own rates-based frame, with the population/immigration measurement
    argument doing the "kept pace with the workforce" work in his own
    words: payroll gains are unreadable "because there are major question
    marks about population growth. A lot coming from immigration, some
    coming from labor force participation."

Own verdict (verbatim repeat, third consecutive appearance):
  "My read of the job market is it's been remarkably stable without being
   good. So it's not a super boom, but it's not falling apart."
  → Identical characterization to May 12 and Jun 25. UNCHANGED. (Media
    accounts of this event rendered it "stable without being strong" —
    the transcript reads "stable without being good," his standing idiom.)

Own framework (repeat — the four rates, unnamed this time):
  "the unemployment rate's been stable and all the rates-based measures,
   the hiring rate, the layoff rate, the vacancy rate, the unemployment
   rate, those have been largely flat. And I think those are the best
   indicators of business cycle."
  → Same four-indicator frame as May 12/Jun 25 (the "four horsemen" label
    itself was not used). Explicitly demotes payrolls: the monthly jobs
    number "has broken down as the leading indicator of the business
    cycle." [Inference: this is his look-through of the weak June NFP
    (+57K) — weak payroll prints are measurement noise so long as the
    four rates stay flat; he does not cite the June print directly.]

Own qualifier — the paradox (repeat):
  "if you just took the hiring rate, it's as low as it is at the depths
   of a recession... But at the same time the layoff rate is so low that
   you would think we're at the peak of a giant boom... that's a weird
   combination. That's not a normal business cycle combination. I think
   it's because there's a lot of uncertainty."
  → Same low-hire/low-fire paradox, same uncertainty attribution, same
    new-graduate distributional point as prior two appearances.
  Forward view: "on the job side we're still going to see that" —
   stability expected to persist.

Regional color: "Wisconsin is kind of in labor shortage mode... it never
  actually got better... it's still a really tight labor market" —
  district-level tightness, cuts against any labor-fragility narrative.

Shift vs. prior speech (Jun 25):  UNCHANGED — verdict, framework, causal
  story, and forward view are materially identical.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (May 12):      UNCHANGED.

INFLATION
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "supply shocks that have driven price increases in certain sectors,
   including energy" [Jun 17 stmt]
  → Speaker version: same layered-shock narrative in his own words:
    "we were making progress from 3%... we were close to 2% for a
    10-month period... Then we stopped making progress. The tariffs came
    in. That was supposed to go away... Then the war started, the price
    of oil went up before that one had gone away."

Own language — June CPI reaction (same-day):
  "it was surprisingly benign and inflation was lower than what it has
   been running... it was a bit cutting the other way from what things
   have been for the last six months or so."
  Discount rule (verbatim repeat from Jun 25): "I always say one month is
   no months. You know, you never want to overreact to one month. If we
   got several months like this, I would be feeling better."
  Closing restatement: "I'm heartened by this CPI today, but we need a
   lot more than one month to think that it's going well."
  → Welcomes the print, refuses to bank it. The multi-month bar is
    applied symmetrically to good news, exactly as he applied it to the
    single favorable goods-CPI print on Jun 25 and to a potential energy
    decline ("don't overreact on the way down").

Own language — KEY (services persistence, maintained):
  "the fact that services inflation has been way too high — historically
   services inflation is quite persistent. And so that's what I've been
   highlighting. That number has been making me nervous. At least today,
   at least for this one, the services inflation was very encouraging."
  → The services-persistence concern (held since May 12) is intact; the
    June print gets one favorable data-point acknowledgment within it.
    Tariffs remain the component he can look through ("tariffs should be
    a one-time increase in prices but not a lasting inflation rate
    increase. That should be one and done"); services remain the
    component he cannot.

Own language — persistence conversation (maintained from Jun 25):
  "it was coming down, then it stopped going down, and then lately it's
   been going the wrong way. And it's way too high, going the wrong way.
   And now we got to start having a conversation about is inflation going
   to be more persistent than what we wanted it to be... That for me has
   been the thing I've been trying to grapple with."
  → The persistence question remains his stated central concern, same as
    Jun 25 ("inflation proves substantially more persistent than we are
    currently forecasting").

Data sequencing: names PPI explicitly as the next gate: "We got to see
  what those producer prices... we'll get that tomorrow," and notes CPI
  "is not exactly the measure we usually look at" — PCE is the bar.

Shift vs. prior speech (Jun 25):  unchanged framework, marginal dovish
  tone — same services concern, same one-month discount rule, but this
  time the incoming data point is favorable and he says so ("heartened,"
  "very encouraging"). The bar itself did not move.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (May 12):      dovish lean in tone — May 12 was a
  fresh-CPI alarm ("going the wrong way," "unexpectedly disappointing");
  this is a fresh-CPI acknowledgment of progress under the same
  framework.

MONETARY POLICY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed / committee process:
  No easing-bias language exists in the Jun 17 statement to echo, and he
  echoes none. His no-forecast posture tracks the Warsh communications
  regime he endorsed on Jun 25: "I have never been a huge fan of
  forecasting here's what the interest rate is going to do even at the
  next meeting. I don't like even implicitly tying our hands or putting
  weight on the scale. I want to see what the data are coming in and I
  want to hear from the other of the 19 people sitting around the table."
  → Declines the direct rate-path question. Consistent with the
    forward-guidance-free regime; treat as committee-consensus alignment.

Own language — KEY (conditional normalization path):
  "if we got PCE versions of inflation that look like what the CPI
   inflation was today and we got that for several months and it was
   clear we're back on a path toward 2% inflation, which is our target,
   I would be feeling a lot better. And I felt like before we got some of
   what I was calling the stagflationary dust in the air, we were on path
   to get to 2%. And the sooner we can show that we're headed back to 2%,
   I think the sooner we can normalize."
  → The most consequential line of the event, and absent from all media
    accounts. He volunteers a named, data-conditional path back to cuts:
    several months of CPI-like PCE prints → "normalize." This is the
    dovish-side counterpart to Waller's Jul 13 hike conditionality — the
    committee now has explicit conditional branches on both sides from
    different officials, each keyed to the same near-term inflation data.
    [Inference: "normalize" implies he views the current stance as above
    neutral — in tension with his Feb 24 "not obvious that our interest
    rate policy is even restrictive." Not stated directly; the word
    choice is doing the work.]
  Scenario framing: descriptive-conditional — a data path with a policy
    direction attached, but no named scenarios and no pre-commitment on
    timing or magnitude. Weaker than Waller's explicit rule; stronger
    than pure risk description.

On Chair Warsh (asked directly):
  "he's levelheaded... he has expressed different ideas about
   communication, about forward guidance and the dot plot... the balance
   sheet. It's, I think, healthy for institutions to go through
   re-examinations periodically... I've been impressed."
  Task forces: "He's announced a series of task forces with really
   pretty people of high stature from outside the Fed to think through
   issues like inflation, AI and productivity, the balance sheet, and I'm
   going to be interested to see what comes out of those."
  → Collegial ratification of the Warsh regime, consistent with Jun 25
    ("foxhole buddy," "caffeine cleanse"). Independently corroborates the
    task-force topics Warsh gave in the Jul 14 House testimony
    (inflation frameworks, AI/productivity, balance sheet). Process
    commentary, not a rate signal.

Cut signal status: CONDITIONAL — restored. May 12: "rates can come down
  still a fair amount" (conditional). Jun 25: absent. Jul 14: "the sooner
  we can show that we're headed back to 2%... the sooner we can
  normalize" — a conditional normalization path, more guarded than
  May 12 (no "fair amount" magnitude) but a directional cut path
  nonetheless. No hike language anywhere; Waller's Jul 13 tightening
  conditionality is not engaged.

Shift vs. prior speech (Jun 25):  dovish lean — reintroduces a
  conditional normalization/cut path where Jun 25 had none, and the
  productivity-overheating risk is not reprised.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (May 12):      hawkish lean — the cut signal survives
  only in weakened form ("normalize" on a multi-month PCE condition vs.
  "come down still a fair amount"), consistent with the intervening
  June 17 hawkish consensus.

SUMMARY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Net signal:    DOVISH LEAN vs. Jun 25, entirely data-conditional.
               Labor is unchanged (third consecutive "stable without
               being good"). Inflation framework is unchanged — services
               persistence is still the worry, one month is still no
               months — but the incoming print is favorable and he says
               so. The move is in monetary policy: he volunteers a
               conditional normalization path (several months of
               CPI-like PCE → "the sooner we can normalize") that was
               absent on Jun 25. Against the committee: he is the most
               receptive official to the benign June CPI, but his
               multi-month PCE bar means the print changes his mood, not
               his stance. July 28–29: nothing here resists a hold.

Language watch:
  • "the sooner we can show that we're headed back to 2%, the sooner we
     can normalize" — conditional normalization path; the dovish
     counterpart to Waller's Jul 13 hike conditionality. Watch whether
     any voter adopts a symmetric two-sided conditional frame. Stage:
     DISPERSED (single official).
  • "one month is no months" — Goolsbee idiom, second consecutive
     appearance, now applied to a favorable print. Stable personal idiom.
  • "stable without being good" — third consecutive appearance; his
     fixed labor verdict. Media rendered it "stable without being
     strong" — analyst should treat the transcript version as canonical.
  • "stagflationary dust in the air" — his idiom for the tariff+oil
     shock period, self-cited ("what I was calling"); marks his framing
     that the pre-shock disinflation path was intact.
  • Payrolls "broken down as the leading indicator of the business
     cycle" — rates-based measures preferred; consistent committee-wide
     drift away from payroll prints (cf. Daly Jul 2, Warsh Sintra).

Analyst flag: YES
  → (1) The "sooner we can normalize" conditional is the first
    post-June-17 language from any official sketching the dovish exit
    ramp. Read against Waller's Jul 13 hike conditionality: both keyed to
    the same near-term inflation data (PPI Jul 15, then PCE), pointing in
    opposite directions. Assess whether this is Goolsbee's cut path
    reopening (after Jun 25 silence) or venue-driven softness.
  → (2) "Normalize" implies above-neutral policy — tension with his
    Feb 24 "not obvious rates are even restrictive." Probe at his next
    directly policy-focused appearance.
