GROWTH

  → Speaker version: "very strong investment growth, exceedingly strong
    investment growth... in AI, and it's driven a lot [by] physical
    construction, but also investment in... AI equipment... computers and

Own language:

Shift vs. PRE-FOMC BASELINE (May 8):    The May 8 speech was framed almost

  comparable growth assessment; this is a materially fuller growth section,
  emphasizing AI-capex strength as the primary growth engine. Direction:
  HAWKISH-LEAN new information (structural growth resilience, echoing
  Warsh's June 17 and July 1 productivity framing — see LANGUAGE WATCH).

INFLATION
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed: June 17 — "remains elevated relative to the
  Committee's 2 percent goal, in part reflecting supply shocks."
  → Speaker version: "we have inflation still printing above target. In
    fact, inflation's risen on the back of some of the still the tariff
    effects and also the oil price shock, but now with oil prices back down
    to near 70 and futures markets thinking they'll stay there, that has
    some hope for relief."

Own language — KEY:
  "I'm say[ing] this is good for the United States growth rate, it's good
  for the US labor market, and we continue to have policy in a slightly
  restrictive position, so inflation should come down." — explicit
  restrictiveness self-assessment (consistent with the July 3 report's
  provisional "Policy slightly restrictive" quote, now confirmed verbatim
  in context) combined with a constructive near-term inflation call.

Explicit scenario framing — DESCRIPTIVE, not Waller-style named
  pre-commitment (per CLAUDE.md distinction):
  "There's a scenario where we have to fight inflation that turns out to be
  more persistent. There's also a scenario where the growth just doesn't
  continue to sustain itself... Those are not more likely than the [modal
  outlook], but they are the risks that we face." — two risk scenarios
  named (persistent-inflation / growth-pullback) but NEITHER is attached to
  a specific policy response — purely descriptive risk-flagging, not an
  explicit pre-committed reaction like Waller's Apr 17 BENIGN/ADVERSE
  framework.

Shift vs. PRE-FOMC BASELINE (May 8):    May 8 ("dashboard... starting to see

  risk than this speech, which treats the oil retreat to ~$70 as a
  stabilizing, "hopeful" development. Direction: DOVISH-LEAN relative to
  May 8 — the oil-shock red flag from the dashboard speech has faded.

MONETARY POLICY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed: policy stance described as "slightly
  restrictive," consistent with the committee's hold at 3.50–3.75%.

Own language — NOTABLE DIVERGENCE FROM THE CHAIR:
  "That's why you see a lot of central bankers not moving away from the
  principle [of forward guidance]. I will speak for myself: I don't move
  away from the principle about forward guidance. I understand its
  importance in monetary policy, but... the worst kind of guidance you can
  give is certain guidance in an uncertain world. And so, that would be
  false guidance and not forward guidance." — this reframes the current
  "no forward guidance" posture as a response to uncertainty, NOT a
  rejection of forward guidance as a tool. This is subtly but genuinely
  different from Warsh's June 17/July 1 framing, which characterizes forward
  guidance as structurally "not well suited" and something he has "long
  written about" wanting to move away from on principle. [Inference: Daly is
  not contradicting the committee's current practice, but her underlying
  rationale differs from the Chair's — worth flagging as a language-cycle
  data point: is "no forward guidance" committee-wide doctrine (Warsh's
  framing) or a shared tactical response to unusual uncertainty (Daly's
  framing)? Not yet resolved; watch other officials' framing.]

  On decision frequency: explicitly declined to speculate — "I'm not going
  to speak for the Federal Reserve here, and certainly anything like that
  hasn't been discussed." — directly relevant given Warsh's June 17 hint
  about reducing press-conference frequency; Daly does not confirm or amplify
  that direction.

  On pace: "I'm much more of a gradualist when it comes to these things and
  taking it slowly to do it well." — self-described gradualism, consistent
  with "slightly restrictive, patient" positioning; no urgency in either
  direction.

  On the Strait of Hormuz / Middle East risk: "hopefully that is fully
  resolved... geopolitical issues are something central bankers don't
  control... we take the economy that we have." — declines to escalate
  concern; treats it as an exogenous risk to monitor, not a policy trigger.

Cut/hike signal status: ABSENT. No rate-path signal offered in either
  direction; framing is entirely about maintaining the current "slightly
  restrictive" stance while data resolves the two named scenarios.

Shift vs. PRE-FOMC BASELINE (May 8):    May 8 was watchful/dashboard-driven
  with an implicit hawkish lean (oil shock flashing red, tariff look-through
  intact but conditional). This speech is calmer — the oil dashboard signal
  has faded (~$70 vs. the earlier spike), and the framing is more balanced
  between the two risk scenarios. Direction: SLIGHT DOVISH-LEAN relative to
  May 8, though "slightly restrictive" stance itself is unchanged.
Shift vs. POST-FOMC PRIOR:              N/A.

SPECIAL TOPICS (Type B)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
AI: Sees the US in "the very early stages" of an AI-driven productivity
  cycle, explicitly analogized to electrification's slow-build-then-
  exponential curve; reports a shift in her nine-Western-states district
  from "doom sayers vs. enthusiasts" polarization to broad-based adoption
  ("now there's a lot of people in the middle... not just consumers, I mean
  firms"). On financing/financial-stability risk: notes AI capex is
  currently financed mostly through equity and operating cash flow rather
  than debt — "I haven't seen that as a major concern yet" — but flags
  leverage as the variable to monitor going forward. [Descriptive risk
  framing, not a named policy response.]

HOUSING: Extensive discussion (the conference's core theme). "Housing supply
  is limited... this is a problem the central bank cannot solve... but it is
  a problem I hear... second to inflation" at US conferences. Notes housing
  inflation has been coming down in the US even as pent-up demand persists —
  an implicit disinflationary tailwind distinct from the energy/tariff
  channels. Traces the US demand/supply wedge back to the 2008 financial
  crisis construction slowdown, paralleling Escrivá's Spain-specific
  supply-constraint diagnosis (construction-sector productivity, permitting
  delays, regulatory density).

MANDATE / MEASUREMENT: Pushed back gently, without naming Warsh, on any
  suggestion the Fed would redefine its mandate in response to AI-era
  uncertainty: "no, we're not moving the goal posts, but we definitely will
  continue to study, modernize, and do better tomorrow than we did today." —
  a notably more conservative framing than Warsh's task-force language
  (which explicitly questions whether current data/measurement practices are
  adequate); Daly frames improvement as continuous refinement, not a
  first-principles review. [Inference: this is not a direct contradiction of
  Warsh's task forces, but a different emphasis — worth tracking whether
  Daly's tone shifts once the task forces report.]

DECISION CADENCE / GRADUALISM: On whether faster-changing conditions should
  mean more frequent policy decisions: "You don't want to react quickly when
  the world is changing quickly. You want to assess before you jump... I'm
  much more of a gradualist." — directly counters any inference that
  Warsh's institutional-reform push implies faster or more reactive
  decision-making; Daly's framing is explicitly the opposite emphasis
  (patience under uncertainty, not speed).

SUMMARY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Net signal:    NEUTRAL-TO-SLIGHT-DOVISH-LEAN vs. her May 8 pre-FOMC baseline;
               broadly consistent with, but not identical in rationale to,
               the June 17 committee stance.

  Daly reaffirms the "slightly restrictive" stance and a steady labor market,
  and — like Warsh a day earlier at Sintra — explicitly pre-committed to
  discount the imminent June payrolls print on multi-indicator grounds
  minutes before it landed weak. Her May 8 oil-shock "dashboard" alarm has
  faded now that oil has retreated to ~$70; that is the main dovish delta
  versus her own prior speech. The most analytically significant finding is
  not a directional shift but a RATIONALE DIVERGENCE from the Chair: Daly
  defends forward guidance "in principle" as valuable, framing its current
  absence as a response to uncertainty ("false guidance... not forward
  guidance"), whereas Warsh frames forward guidance as structurally
  ill-suited and something he has long wanted to retire. Both land on the
  same practice (no forward guidance now) via different doctrines — this is
  a live language-cycle question, not yet resolved by convergence.

Language watch:
  • "One data point is not going to make or break our analysis" — stated
    just ahead of the June NFP miss; near-identical to Warsh's Sintra
    framing the day before ("we follow trends... not recent events"). Watch
    whether this becomes explicit shared committee language for handling the
    weak print in upcoming communications (Minutes, July FOMC).
  • "That would be false guidance and not forward guidance" — Daly's
    defense-in-principle of forward guidance; watch whether this framing is
    echoed by other officials or stays an outlier against Warsh's more
    absolute rejection.
  • "We're not moving the goal posts" — implicit, unprompted contrast with
    the tenor of Warsh's data/measurement task forces; watch for tension or
    convergence as the task forces report (expected week of July 6).

Analyst flag: YES
  → (1) The forward-guidance rationale gap (Daly: response to uncertainty;
    Warsh: structural rejection) is worth tracking across the next several
    Just Voter and Non Voter speeches — if others adopt Daly's "false
    guidance" framing rather than Warsh's structural critique, it suggests
    the no-forward-guidance shift is more tactical/consensus-driven than the
    Chair's personal reform agenda implies.
  → (2) Both Warsh and Daly pre-committed publicly to discount the June
    payrolls print within 24 hours of its release, before either had seen
    it. Now that the print came in weak with an offsetting unemployment-rate
    decline and wage acceleration (see 20260703_daily.txt), track whether
    officials actually hold to that discipline or selectively cite the
    print's hawkish components (falling unemployment, rising wages) while
    calling the miss itself noise.
