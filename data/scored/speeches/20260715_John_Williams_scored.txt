GROWTH
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Economic activity is expanding at a solid pace" [Jun 17 stmt]
  → Williams: "Growth in the economy is solid and on trend."

Own language:
  "For the past year and a half, we've seen solid GDP growth, at around
  2 percent. There are pockets of red-hot growth fueled by optimism,
  productivity increases, and strong investment. But the economy has been only
  growing near its trend rate because weakness in other sectors has been
  offsetting these gains."
  → Compositional framing: AI/technology optimism, business investment, and
    equity-wealth-supported consumer spending are offsetting "declines in
    spending in other sectors, such as residential construction, expenditures
    by the federal government, and the effects of higher energy costs on
    household spending." The aggregate is solid, but he explicitly names the
    weak legs underneath it — a two-sided composition read.

  Forecast: real GDP "around 2 to 2-1/4 percent this year and over the next
  two years," growth "running modestly above my estimate of its potential rate
  of 2 percent," unemployment "to edge down very gradually to 4 percent in 2028."
  → Same path as June 25 (which gave "around 2-1/4 percent"); the July 15
    range's lower bound (2 percent) is trivially wider, not a downgrade.

Shift vs. prior speech (June 25):  unchanged — same solid, AI-investment-led,
  slightly-above-potential path. The offsetting-weakness composition (housing,
  federal spending, energy drag on households) is fuller than June 25's framing
  but describes the same economy.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (May 4):        unchanged — consistent across three speeches.

LABOR MARKET
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "the unemployment rate has changed little" / "kept pace with the workforce"
  [Jun 17 stmt]
  → Williams: "The unemployment rate has stayed within a narrow range of about
    4-1/4 to 4-1/2 percent over the past year. Payroll employment growth has
    been positive."

Own verdict:
  "we're seeing similar signs of resilience and stability" (topic opening);
  "the labor market is likewise solid and stable" (policy section).
  → "Solid and stable" is his summary verdict for the topic as a whole.

Subsection detail — survey/soft data:
  "survey measures of job and worker availability, along with the New York
  Fed's 'job security gap' — all of which previously flashed warning signs —
  have also largely stabilized, albeit at low levels."
  → This is the same formulation, applied to the same indicators, as June 25.
    It is a subsection characterization of the soft data specifically, not the
    overall labor verdict. The May 4 "increasing labor market slack" concern
    remains withdrawn.

Not a source of inflation pressure (stated in the inflation section):
  "we do not see evidence of the labor market adding to inflationary
  pressures. This is supported by the New York Fed's HPW Labor Market
  Tightness Index and the data on wage growth, which are consistent with low
  inflation."
  → Maintains his stable formulation from June 25 — labor is a non-issue for
    inflation, removing the labor leg as a two-sided risk driver.

Shift vs. prior speech (June 25):  unchanged — same verdict ("solid and
  stable" vs. June 25's "resilient … stable"), same soft-data language
  ("largely stabilized, albeit at low levels"), same not-adding-to-inflation
  anchor.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (May 4):        HAWKISH LEAN (carried forward from June 25)
  — May 4's "increasing labor market slack" soft-data concern remains
  withdrawn; stability is the holding verdict.

INFLATION
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Inflation remains elevated relative to the Committee's 2 percent goal,
   in part reflecting supply shocks including energy" [Jun 17 stmt]
  → Williams: "Inflation is unquestionably too high at about 4 percent, well
    above the FOMC's longer-run goal of 2 percent." (Footnoted as PCE. Note:
    Cook, same day, puts the targeted index at 3.7 percent through June —
    Williams's "about 4 percent" is a rounder characterization of the same
    price picture.)
  Three drivers, unchanged from June 25: (1) tariffs on imported goods;
  (2) Middle East supply-chain disruptions and higher energy/commodity prices;
  (3) "robust demand for certain categories of goods and electricity
  associated with the surge in technology investment." On AI: "we're in a
  race between available supply and surging demand … sharp increases in the
  prices of semiconductors, power transformers, and other technology."

Own language — KEY:
  "There are encouraging reasons to expect that inflation has peaked and
   should edge down in coming quarters."
  → "Peaked" does not appear in the June 25 speech, which said only "I expect
    inflation readings to edge down." July 15 claims the high point has
    already passed — a more confident disinflation call, delivered the same
    morning as the benign June PPI (-0.3% headline; core +0.2%) and the day
    after the benign June CPI (core flat MoM).

Six stated reasons for the peak call:
  1. Tariffs: "the direct effects of existing tariffs on prices appear to have
     in large part played out"; new tariffs "will primarily replace those that
     were curtailed or will soon expire" — no significant additional impulse.
  2. Shelter: "relatively modest increases in market rents" imply shelter
     inflation stays on its three-year downward trajectory.
  3. Energy: "based on oil prices today and futures market pricing into next
     year, it appears that prices for energy and related goods have likely
     peaked and will come down closer to levels seen before the initial
     closure of the Strait of Hormuz" — explicitly flagged as "fluid and
     subject to a great deal of uncertainty."
  4. AI: supply-demand imbalances "should recede over time as more supply
     comes online," though magnitude and duration "highly uncertain."
  5. Labor: not adding to inflationary pressures (HPW index, wage data).
  6. Expectations: "medium- and longer-term inflation expectations remain well
     anchored" — one- and three-year-ahead up modestly, five-year-ahead
     "essentially unchanged since the onset of the Middle East conflict,"
     consistent with market-based measures.
  → Reasons 1, 2, 5, and 6 carry over from June 25; the energy-peak call
    (futures-based) and the explicit AI supply-response argument are firmer
    than June 25's versions of those legs.

Glide path (revised):
  "I expect overall inflation to decline to around 3-1/4 percent by year-end,
   then continue on a glide path toward our 2 percent goal in 2027 and land on
   target in 2028."
  → Year-end 2026 revised down 0.25pp from June 25's "3-1/2 percent by
    year-end." The 2028 return-to-target date is unchanged from June 25.

Shift vs. prior speech (June 25):  DOVISH — "peaked" is a stronger claim than
  "edge down"; year-end forecast revised down 0.25pp (3.5% → 3.25%). Both
  moves absorb the benign June CPI/PPI prints.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (May 4):        MIXED, net mild dovish — the peak call is
  well beyond May 4 (which had inflation risks tilted to the upside and no
  peak claim); but the return-to-2% date (2028) remains a year later than
  May 4's 2027. June 25 bridged the two; July 15 adds the peak claim.

MONETARY POLICY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "The Committee decided to maintain the target range … 3-1/2 to 3-3/4
   percent … in support of the Federal Reserve's dual mandate." [Jun 17 stmt]
  → Williams: "at its meeting in mid-June, the FOMC decided to maintain the
    target range for the federal funds rate at 3-1/2 to 3-3/4 percent in
    support of the Fed's dual mandate."

Own language:
  "with inflation running high, it is imperative that we restore it to the
   Federal Reserve's 2 percent longer-run goal on a sustained basis. The
   current stance of monetary policy is well positioned to do that."
  → Identical stance formulation to June 25 ("imperative … sustained basis" +
    "well positioned"). No cut signal. No hike signal. No forward guidance.
  Closing: "my views, as always, will be based on the evolution of the
  totality of the data, the economic outlook, and the balance of risks" —
  his standing data-dependence formula, verbatim from June 25.
  Risk paragraph: AI-surge effects "hard to predict"; Middle East supply
  disruptions "a source of risk to the outlooks for both growth and inflation."

NOTABLE ABSENCE: Williams does not echo Cook's "prepared to act" (same day)
  or Waller's July 13 near-term tightening consideration. The "peaked" call
  functions as his justification for why the current rate is sufficient —
  disinflation arrives at the current stance — not as a pivot toward cuts.
  [Inference: this is the patient-hold reading of the same data the
  conditional-hike camp treats as merely one print; not stated as a
  disagreement with anyone.]

Cut signal status: ABSENT — unchanged since the April 16 retraction; the
  glide path is described without any rate-cut timing attached.
Hike signal: ABSENT — "well positioned" carries the stance.

Shift vs. prior speech (June 25):  unchanged on stance — patient hold, "well
  positioned," no guidance. The peak call and forecast revision are
  informational updates, not stance changes.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (May 4):        unchanged on stance.
