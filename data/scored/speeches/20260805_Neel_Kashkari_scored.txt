THE HEADLINE FINDING — CONVICTION IS LOWER THAN THE WRITTEN STATEMENT IMPLIED

Two disclosures with no equivalent in the July 31 statement:

  "In this case, I was undecided. I wanted to go into the meeting and hear all
   the arguments on both sides, and I ultimately decided that raising rates was
   the right thing to do."

  "I wasn't pounding the table that we needed to raise now, and if we waited
   till September, that would be too late. But my conclusion based on everything
   I listened to was that it's better to get going."

→ He went into the July 29 meeting UNDECIDED, and he explicitly denies that
  waiting until September would be too late. The July 31 written statement reads
  as settled doctrine ("I increasingly believe"); the spoken version reveals a
  marginal call made in the room.
→ This materially lowers the information content of his dissent as a September
  predictor. A dissenter who was undecided a week earlier and who concedes that
  September would not be too late is a soft vote for a September hike, not a
  committed one. It also fits the July 31 scored file's read that his hedging
  ("increasingly believe") was the weakest of the three dissenters.
→ [Inference: this is the single most decision-relevant sentence of the week for
  September odds. He does not frame it as a softening; the reading is mine.]

GROWTH

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
INFLATION
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Own language — KEY, AND A MATERIAL CLARIFICATION OF THE DISSENT:
  "I don't think most of the inflation that we've experienced is because of
   economic growth and robust demand. I think most of the inflation has been
   because of these successive supply shocks. There is a demand element on top
   of it as well. And nonetheless, it's the Fed's job to get inflation back down
   to our 2% target."
  → This SUBORDINATES demand explicitly. His July 31 statement named data
    centers as "a new demand element"; here he weights it as secondary and
    concedes that MOST of the inflation is supply-driven.
  → This separates him from Hammack and Logan, who argue the residual after
    supply shocks is demand. Kashkari agrees with the majority on the DIAGNOSIS
    and dissents on the PRESCRIPTION: supply-driven or not, persistence obliges
    the Fed to act. It is a doctrinal dissent, not an empirical one — exactly as
    the July 31 file assessed, now confirmed in his own words.
  → Practical consequence: soft inflation prints do not refute his position the
    way they would refute a demand-overheating argument. But his own "undecided"
    admission cuts the other way. These two facts pull in opposite directions
    and the analyst should hold both.

The look-through exit ramp — restated and given its full form:
  "This is the conundrum that we're facing. Traditional economic analysis says
   if there's a one-time supply shock, monetary policy should just let that flow
   through the economy. Don't necessarily react to it. But now we've had a
   series of successive supply shocks over four or five years. Inflation has
   been frustratingly persistent. It's our job to get inflation back down."
  → Verbatim continuity with the July 31 statement's central claim. The
    one-time/successive distinction is the whole argument.

THE 1970s / BURNS ANALOGY — ESCALATED FROM THE WRITTEN STATEMENT:
  "I went back to the transcripts from the 1970s. In the 1970s, the Burns Fed
   also diagnosed the high inflation as a series of successive supply shocks. …
   Now, this is not the 1970s. The economy is way better than it was in the
   1970s on many dimensions. But I don't think anybody would look back and say,
   'Boy, the Burns Fed was right to just look through all those supply shocks and
   let inflation continue to run hot.'"
  → On July 31 the analogy was asserted; here he sources it to primary documents
    ("I went back to the transcripts"). Naming Burns on live television is a
    sharper charge than a written statement's allusion — it accuses the current
    majority of a specific, named historical error.
  → The hedge is retained and is doing real work: "this is not the 1970s … way
    better … on many dimensions."
  → NOTE THE SEQUENCE. The July 31 scored file flagged: "Watch for pushback; if
    a Troika member rebuts it explicitly, that tells you it landed." Paulson —
    the median voter, not Troika — rebutted the look-through argument by name on
    August 4. Kashkari escalated it on August 5. This is now an open, named
    exchange conducted through CNBC over consecutive mornings.

Energy / geopolitics:
  "Energy prices remain high. I think the outlook for the Strait of Hormuz is
   uncertain. I certainly don't trust the Iranians."
  → An upside-risk framing on the shock itself. Contrast Cook (Aug 5), whose
    disinflation channel #2 assumes oil falls by year-end on forecaster
    consensus, and Paulson (Aug 4), who cites the oil-driven June PCE relief as
    look-through validation. Three officials, three different oil assumptions, in
    48 hours.

Shift vs. July 31 statement:  DOVISH LEAN on attribution, unchanged on
  conclusion — conceding that most inflation is supply-driven and demand is
  secondary is a step back from the written statement's demand-channel emphasis,
  though it does not touch his policy recommendation.

MONETARY POLICY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Own language — the dissent restated:
  "at some point the Fed's job is to get inflation back down to our 2% target …
   I argued now is the time to start slowly moving up as we get more data in."

Path — the sequence framing, carried forward and EXTENDED:
  "I'm not calling for a dramatic increase in interest rates. I'm simply saying I
   don't see evidence that monetary policy is [marginally] restrictive right now,
   and I think we have more work to do to get inflation back down. And I would
   rather get going now in small steps than wait till later, when we have a
   really entrenched inflation problem and have to raise rates aggressively
   then."
  → Insurance framing, verbatim in substance with July 31. He remains the only
    official on the committee arguing for a SEQUENCE rather than a single move.
  → ⚠ The word rendered "[marginally]" is an ASR uncertainty — he says
    "particularly restrictive" earlier in the same interview, and Paulson had
    said "mildly restrictive" the day before. Verify against video before
    quoting. The substance (he denies the stance is restrictive) is unambiguous
    either way.

THREE HIKES ENTERTAINED — the most aggressive path any official has voiced:
  Asked about Brian Moynihan's team expecting three hikes by year-end:
  → "that's not impossible in my view. I mean, if inflation continues to move
    sideways or even get worse from here, then I think we're going to have to
    start gradually adjusting interest rates to bring things back down."
  → Strictly conditional and he does not endorse the forecast — but "not
    impossible" from a sitting voter is the outer edge of the committee's
    published option space. New; nothing in the July 31 statement quantifies the
    path.

September — explicitly declines to predict, and lowers the urgency:
  "I'm not going to speculate on what the committee might do. We have a lot of
   data that we're going to get in. We have two more inflation prints between now
   and September."
  → Read against "if we waited till September, that would be too late" (denied):
    his own framing makes the two intervening prints decisive, not his doctrine.

Balance sheet — raised and set aside:
  "Some people view that the federal funds rate and the balance sheet can be
   interchangeable tools … if you believe that, then even if we were to shrink
   the balance sheet, that would also be tapping the brakes. Our primary policy
   tool is the federal funds rate, and we need to focus on our primary policy
   tool."
  → Declines the balance sheet as an alternative tightening route. Relevant
    because the July 29 implementation note was unchanged and the ample-reserves
    language was the meeting's only edit.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
INSTITUTIONAL — WARSH, DISSENT NORMS, AND COMMUNICATION
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
On the new Chair's tolerance of dissent (first-hand, and new to the record):
  "he said publicly many times that he likes a good family fight. … And I'll just
   say, to me, he said to me, 'Do what you think is the right thing to do for the
   economy.' And I said, 'I really appreciate that.'"
  → Directly relevant to how much signal to read into vote counts under Warsh. If
    the Chair actively licenses dissent, a 9–3 understates consensus less than it
    would have under a consensus-managing chair — or, put the other way, future
    dissents are cheaper and therefore weaker signals. Worth carrying into the
    minutes read.

On the pre-meeting call tradition:
  "Chairman Warsh has continued the tradition of reaching out to everybody in
   advance. We have a discussion about how I'm seeing the economy, how he's
   seeing the economy … And then I say, here's where I'm likely coming out."

On communication — DIVERGES FROM THE CHAIR:
  "I personally think that communicating what we call our reaction function — so
   not necessarily making predictions about what we think will happen, but
   saying, here's some number of different scenarios: if these economic events
   happen, this is how one would expect inflation and monetary policy to respond
   — I think that is good for market participants to understand." Pressed on
   whether that is more or less information: "I think there's value in continuing
   the tradition of explaining our reaction function to the public."
  → Warsh argued at the July 29 press conference that Fed communication needs
    rolling back. Kashkari defends reaction-function communication. Note the
    contrast with Paulson (Aug 4), who narrowed "forward guidance" to a ZLB tool
    and declined to defend Fed communication in general. On the reform agenda the
    median voter is accommodating and the dissenter is resisting.

On six meetings: "I don't think there's any magic number about eight or 10 or
  six. … I'm open-minded. I don't have a strong view."

SUMMARY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Net signal:    HAWKISH in position, MATERIALLY SOFTER in conviction than the
               July 31 written statement. The headline — "now is the time to
               start slowly moving" — is continuous with the dissent. What is
               new, and what moves the September read, is everything around it:
               he was UNDECIDED going into the meeting, he denies that waiting
               until September would have been too late, and he concedes that
               MOST of the inflation is supply-driven with demand only "on top
               of it."

               Set against that, the path he entertains got longer, not shorter:
               three hikes by year-end is "not impossible," and he remains the
               only official arguing for a sequence rather than a move. The
               correct reading is a dissenter who is firm about direction, soft
               about timing, and unusually open about how close the call was.

               His argument survives soft inflation data by construction — it is
               doctrinal, resting on the persistence of successive supply shocks
               rather than on demand overheating. But his own admission that the
               call was marginal, plus his framing of the two intervening prints
               as decisive, means the data can still move him. Both things are
               true and they are in tension.

Position relative to committee:
  Across three consecutive days the committee's three published voices produced a
  legible exchange rather than three separate speeches:
    Aug 4  Paulson (median, majority) — underlying inflation 2.4–2.8%; look
           through supply shocks; the June PCE relief validates doing so; the
           timing argument ("I would have been too late"); "not a close call."
    Aug 5  Cook (majority) — inflation "too high" at 3.7%, but declines to defend
           the statement's supply-shock attribution ("not the only factor");
           three disinflation channels; hike conditional named but weakened.
    Aug 5  Kashkari (dissenter) — no evidence policy is restrictive; most
           inflation IS supply-driven, and that is precisely why look-through has
           failed; Burns.
  The axis is no longer majority-vs-dissenters. Paulson and Kashkari agree the
  inflation is mostly supply-driven and disagree on what follows; Cook disputes
  the supply diagnosis itself while voting with Paulson. Three voters, three
  incompatible routes to two different votes.

Language watch:
  • "a series of successive supply shocks" — the look-through exit ramp, now the
    most contested formulation of the cycle and openly rebutted by the median
    voter. Stage: EMERGING, contested. Highest-priority phrase for the July 28–29
    minutes (Aug 19).
  • The Burns / 1970s analogy — escalated from assertion to sourced claim ("I
    went back to the transcripts"). Stage: DISPERSED but now ANSWERED — Paulson's
    timing rebuttal is the first direct pushback. Watch for a Troika response.
  • "not impossible" (three hikes by year-end) — the outer edge of the published
    option space. Stage: DISPERSED (Kashkari alone).
  • "I was undecided" — not a tracked phrase, but the disclosure that most
    changes September odds.
  • "he likes a good family fight" (on Warsh) — dissent norms under the new
    Chair. Bears directly on how much signal to read into 9–3.
