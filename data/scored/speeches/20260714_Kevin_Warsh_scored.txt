GROWTH
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  June 17 opening: "Productivity growth and capital investment are strong."
  → Prepared remarks: "economic activity is expanding at a solid pace, showing
    resilience in the face of recent developments." Consistent with June 17 framing.

Own language:
  "The most striking feature of the economy right now is business investment. The
  rapid pace — which appears to be accelerating — reflects, in large part, the
  construction of data centers and the immense demand for the AI-related equipment
  and software that fill them." Quantified: equipment investment +8% y/y through Q1;
  high-tech spending "nearly 25 percent on a four-quarter basis."
  → AI investment elevated to THE defining economic feature. "What is now called
    'AI investment' will soon be called just 'investment.'"

  Sector qualifier (NEW vs. media extracts): "The housing sector, however, gives a
  different picture and continues to lag." Repeated in Q&A: "a somewhat more cautious
  note about housing"; 30-yr mortgage rates high "in part... because of inflation
  that has been above the Fed's objectives" — he routes housing weakness back INTO
  the price-stability case rather than treating it as a rate-cut argument.

  Q&A — AI pace (NEW): "It's happening faster than I would have projected 18 months
  or two years ago... Something like a hyper Moore's law is happening." "This is a
  supply shock in the United States." On labor impact so far: "it looks to me as
  though this technology hasn't displaced workers at this point. It's made them a
  bit more productive. And I say a bit because I think the productivity is in front
  of us."

  Q&A — REFUSED the Greenspan analogy (KEY, NEW): Asked whether AI presents the same
  opportunity as the 1990s productivity boom, when Greenspan "cut rates... ahead of
  the data": "If you've seen one productivity boom, you've seen one productivity
  boom... I think this could be that opportunity but I can't say it for certain as
  of yet." — Warsh explicitly declined a congressional invitation to endorse
  preemptive productivity-based rate cuts. [Inference: this closes off, for now, the
  most plausible dovish reading of his own supply-side optimism — that structural
  productivity gains justify easier policy. He accepts the supply-shock diagnosis
  but not the policy conclusion.]

Shift vs. prior speech (Jul 1, Sintra):   UNCHANGED/slightly firmer — Sintra
  quantified "structural productivity in the high 2% range"; testimony elevates AI
  investment to headline status and adds the housing-lag qualifier. The refusal of
  the Greenspan preemptive-cut analogy is new and blocks the dovish reading of his
  productivity optimism.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC:  N/A (no pre-FOMC baseline for Warsh as Chair).

LABOR MARKET
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  June 17: "Job gains have kept pace with the workforce, and the unemployment rate
  has changed little."
  → Prepared remarks: "America's labor market appears broadly stable. Job creation
    has kept pace with the workforce. The unemployment rate is low and has changed
    little over the past year." Near-verbatim statement echo = committee consensus.

Own verdict:
  "Broadly stable" — unchanged from Sintra's "steady." Full detail (NEW): "relatively
  few layoffs, only slight variance in the rate of job vacancies, and solid growth
  in nominal wages." The weak June NFP (+57K, July 2) did NOT move his
  characterization — consistent with Sintra's "follow trends, not recent events."

Own qualifier — Q&A dual-mandate prioritization (KEY, NEW):
  "We look out the window now, the labor markets look to be in pretty good balance.
  We've got some work to do on the inflation front." — the cleanest statement of
  where his attention is: labor requires no policy response; inflation does.
  Framing: "I do not think we have a cruel choice between stable prices and full
  employment... not an either-or proposition." "The more we can do to deliver low
  and stable prices... the more employers are going to want to hire more workers."
  [Inference: the "no cruel choice" doctrine functionally subordinates the
  employment mandate to the inflation fight — full employment is delivered THROUGH
  price stability, so labor softening would not independently justify easing. Not
  stated directly.]
  Also (Torres Q&A): "We have more control over the price level ourselves than we
  do the unemployment rate."

Shift vs. prior speech (Jul 1, Sintra):   UNCHANGED verdict ("steady" → "broadly
  stable"); the "no cruel choice" / labor-fine-inflation-is-the-work prioritization
  is a new, mildly hawkish articulation.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC:  N/A.

INFLATION
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  June 17 stmt: "The Committee will deliver price stability." / "Inflation has been
  running somewhat above the Committee's 2 percent longer-run goal."
  → Prepared remarks: "no tolerance for persistently elevated inflation"; "resolute
    commitment to restoring price stability"; "the inflation surge of the last five
    years will be a thing of the past." Committee-level language (MPR consensus).

Own language — KEY:
  "There might be some that look at this morning's data and say, 'Oh, mission
  accomplished. Everything is swell.' That is not my view." (Q&A, twice — to Steil
  and in variant to Meuser: "I'm not for cherrypicking. I am not going to show up
  here and say mission accomplished.") Full context confirms the framing: "It's one
  data point. The reason I create a task force on data is I don't want to overread
  or cherrypick data."

  "While monthly price fluctuations are inevitable — especially in an unsettled
  world — underlying inflation over longer time horizons is determined largely by
  monetary policy." — subordinates any single print to the multi-period trend.

  "Sticky prices... unstick them" (NEW): "Good economics going back a couple of
  generations talks about sticky prices. And the longer that prices have been above
  the inflation target, it's usually a bit harder to dislodge them and get them
  lower. Our job, my commitment to you is to take sticky prices and to unstick
  them." — hawkish duration framing: the 63-month overshoot makes disinflation
  HARDER, raising the bar for declaring progress.

  "63 months" (NEW): repeated count of inflation above target ("the last 63 months,
  maybe 64 months") — a running clock that hardens the five-years framing into an
  explicit, updating tally.

  2% target and definition of price stability (NEW, Davidson Q&A): "This committee
  had long committed to a 2% inflation target. That is our resolution. That's what
  I'm here to say and double down on. But... my broader definition of price
  stability is a change in prices such that households and businesses don't have to
  worry about it, don't have to think about it... That was a definition from the
  Volcker era... That broader price stability objective is still one in the back of
  my mind and we'll see whether there's further reforms to be had." — 2% explicitly
  reaffirmed; the Volcker-era "don't have to think about it" definition is layered
  on top, with an open door to framework reform. [Inference: if anything, the
  "broader definition" implies a stricter standard than 2%, not a softer one.]

  Trimmed-mean denial (NEW, Torres Q&A): The May 31 WSJ report that he wants greater
  weight on Dallas trimmed-mean PCE "is incorrect that I have a preferred measure
  from one of the reserve banks... none of those are very good measures of
  underlying inflation. My view is that we need new measures." — kills a tracked
  storyline; the data task force is the vehicle.

  2020 FAIT framework (NEW): "It was a mistake. The framework did not succeed in its
  objectives... Had that mistake been called out sooner, I don't believe we would
  have been burdened with this undue tax over the last five years."

  FOMC unanimity claim (NEW, Williams-of-Texas Q&A): "As I looked around the table
  of my 18 colleagues, there was no willingness to tolerate higher prices. There was
  a commitment that was unambiguous and unanimous."

  "Regime change" — confirmed in his own words (not just CNBC paraphrase): "If that
  means we need a regime change in policy and we need new consideration of
  practices, some of which have been working, some of which haven't. That's what we
  aim to do and we're just getting started."

Comparison with Sintra (Jul 1):
  Sintra's tactical softening ("inflation risks have come down," "expectations...
  have come down") has NO echo anywhere in the 3-hour hearing — confirmed against
  the full transcript, not just extracts. The testimony re-establishes the June 17
  hawkish anchor; the Sintra softening was situational (oil-price retreat), not
  structural.

Shift vs. prior speech (Jul 1, Sintra):   HAWKISH LEAN — full hawkish vigilance
  restored; "not mission accomplished" rejects the dovish CPI narrative in real
  time; "sticky prices" adds a duration-based reason to distrust one good print.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC:  N/A.

MONETARY POLICY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  No forward guidance in June 17 stmt (removed).
  → Testimony: no rate-path signal anywhere in 3 hours of Q&A. July 28–29 referenced
    only as "we need to have a continued good family fight on this subject. That'll
    start again in a couple of weeks."

Own language:
  No rate signal — fully consistent with stated no-forward-guidance policy: "You'll
  hear from me a lot less forward guidance and a lot of listening... deeper more
  serious discussions among us, fewer pronouncements." On why: pre-committing means
  "taking information that's consistent with our priors and rejecting information
  that's inconsistent... being somewhat more circumspect in our communications, at
  least for me, is a better way of calling balls and strikes."

  "No anguish in central banking" (NEW): rejecting Arthur Burns's framing: "There's
  no anguish in central banking here, Mr. Chairman. We have the commitment, the
  power, and the responsibility to deliver on the mandate... You will not hear me
  blaming anyone else." Formula repeated: "commitment, ownership, tools — we're
  three for three and we'll deliver."

  BALANCE SHEET DOCTRINE (KEY, NEW — largest block of new signal in the hearing):
  • "The balance sheet is part of monetary policy. Monetary policy is made both
    through interest rates and through the balance sheet policies. The balance
    sheet isn't merely about plumbing."
  • "In normal times, the Federal Reserve should be a price taker, not a price
    maker... We shouldn't have our thumb generally on the scale."
  • Post-crisis: "monetary policy, in my view, should be driven almost exclusively
    by interest rate policy... interest rates get in the cracks" (citing a former
    colleague); "interest rates don't favor one class of people versus another."
  • Duration facts deployed: Fed holdings average ~6.5 years vs. ~4.5 years for
    market-held Treasuries; when holdings are large "monetary policy is, in the
    language of former chairman Volcker, on the edge of its authority."
  • Direction: "I'm not of the mistaken view we can go back to where we were when I
    arrived at the Fed in 2006, but I think there are several other sustainable
    equilibria we can achieve." / "I'm inclined to think that there are better
    regimes we can go to."
  • Process commitment: "If there were a change in balance sheet policy... we would
    preview it, explain it, debate it, and no changes... would happen without good
    advance notice to the likes of this committee and broadly financial markets."
    "It took us nearly 18 years to find our way into this balance sheet... We won't
    be able to make changes overnight."
  [Inference: the doctrine points toward eventual balance-sheet reduction /
  composition shift toward shorter duration, well-telegraphed, framed as regime
  reform rather than active tightening. He is pre-building the communications
  runway. Not a near-term policy signal, but the clearest map yet of where the
  balance-sheet task force lands.]

  QE: "I don't think it's inherently inflationary, especially if we adopt
  quantitative easing in the depths of a crisis." Willing to be "quite aggressive"
  with the balance sheet in crisis; the objection is to persistence beyond crisis.

  Independence — strongest formulations yet (NEW escalation): "The independence of
  the Federal Reserve is sacrosanct." "The Fed will remain independent on my watch."
  Asked what he'd do if targeted by the president: "I would continue to do my job."
  Asked if he works for Trump: "We're an independent central bank... My commitment
  to you is to follow the law and follow the data." Asked if he'd hold even under
  public presidential criticism: "I will, Congressman." — Fourth-plus public
  independence declaration; "sacrosanct" and "on my watch" are the strongest
  formulations to date.

  Task forces: staffed and operating ("in discovery mode"; balance sheet TF led by
  "three distinguished leaders," productivity TF by "three experts" reporting "over
  the coming 3 to 6 months"). Results shared "periodically between now and the end
  of the year at which point I hope we've got some real conclusions." — RESOLVES
  the open Sintra item (membership expected week of July 6): task forces are
  constituted, membership not itemized publicly in this hearing.

Cut signal status: ABSENT (consistent across all post-June-17 Warsh appearances).
Hike conditionality: ABSENT — did NOT echo Waller's July 13 "FOMC will need to
  consider tightening in the near term." Neither endorsed nor rejected. Deliberate
  silence consistent with the orchestrator role.

Shift vs. prior speech (Jul 1, Sintra):   UNCHANGED on rates — consistent no-signal
  posture. NEW: full balance-sheet doctrine on the record; independence language
  escalated to "sacrosanct"/"on my watch."

SUMMARY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Net signal:    NEUTRAL by design, HAWKISH by framing.

  Full-transcript re-score confirms the July 14 read and strengthens it. Warsh gave
  no rate-path signal in three hours of questioning — a demonstration, not just a
  statement, of the no-forward-guidance policy. The hawkish framing is now better
  documented: "not mission accomplished" (twice, unprompted variants), "sticky
  prices... unstick them" (duration makes disinflation harder), the "63 months"
  running clock, "no cruel choice" (labor delivered through price stability), and
  the refusal of the Greenspan preemptive-cut analogy — which together close every
  dovish exit from his own optimistic supply-side story. Labor is "in pretty good
  balance"; inflation is "the work."

  The genuinely new intelligence is the balance-sheet doctrine: part of monetary
  policy, not plumbing; price taker not price maker; interest rates as the
  near-exclusive post-crisis tool; duration gap (6.5y vs 4.5y) explicitly flagged;
  "better regimes we can go to" with a long, well-telegraphed transition. This is
  the clearest preview of the balance-sheet task force's landing zone.

  Notably absent, still: any echo of Waller's July 13 near-term tightening
  conditionality. Warsh holds the center; July 28–29 optionality fully preserved.
