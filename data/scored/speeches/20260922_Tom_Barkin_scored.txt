2026-09-22 | Thomas I. Barkin | Richmond Fed President | Non Voter
"Why Hike?"
CFA Society Baltimore, The Center Club, Baltimore, Md.
SOURCE: https://www.richmondfed.org/press_room/speeches/thomas_i_barkin/2026/barkin_speech_20260922
CLASSIFICATION: Type A — Economic Outlook, organized as "Why hike? /
  Why now? / What's next?" Covers labor, growth (consumer + investment),
  inflation and policy. Barkin's first post-September-16 speech and the
  most detailed defense of the hike by any participant other than the
  Chair (Schmid endorsed it first, briefly, on Sept 18).

  Sept 22 daily as first sent. Fetched directly Sept 23.

GROWTH
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "domestic spending has been resilient. Productivity growth is strong,
   and capital investment is robust." [Sep 16 stmt]
  → Speaker version: "The economy and the labor market remain on solid
    footing. We hear from businesses that economic conditions are, if
    anything, firming." — echoes and pushes one step past the statement:
    "firming" is a direction, where the statement gives a level.
Own language — consumer:
  "Consumer spending makes up about 70 percent of GDP, and consumers are
   still spending. Higher gas prices don't seem to have had much effect.
   We got a blowout retail sales report last week."
  "Spending is disproportionately driven by those who are well off, and
   that group has gotten even wealthier ... even those with fewer
   resources are still finding ways to consume ... trading down to
   private labels ... from beef to chicken to even cheaper proteins, like
   peanut butter ... buying used instead of new ... dropping coverages or
   opting out of insurance ... living a little closer to the edge, but
   they're still spending."
  → The three-tier consumer structure from Aug 13 is intact; the coping
    list is longer and more specific. Compositional concern retained.
Own language — investment:
  "investment is booming ... nearly $700 billion in AI investments were
   announced in just one week. But I am hearing momentum outside of data
   centers, too. The defense sector is hot. Manufacturing contacts are
   starting to sound more upbeat. Bankers tell us pipelines are healthy."
  "Uncertainty was the story last year, but now it seems to have become
   the new baseline. Business leaders say they can't afford to wait for
   certainty any longer."
  → Aug 13's fog-reversal is now stated as settled ("the new baseline").
Own language — the one demand gap:
  "The big gap on the demand side is still hiring. Firms still seem to
   be dragging their feet in the context of increased productivity, the
   potential of AI, and caution about the future."
Verdict: "So, the demand picture hasn't changed much."
Shift vs. prior speech (Aug 13):  unchanged-to-hawkish lean — same
  structure and verdict, with "firming" and the retail-sales reference
  added; the AI-breadth point is repeated with new sectors (defense).
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Aug 13):      same document.

LABOR MARKET
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Job gains have kept pace with the workforce, and the unemployment rate
   has changed little." [Sep 16 stmt]
  → Speaker version: "This has been less of an issue for the unemployment
    rate because workforce growth has slowed as well, due to reduced
    immigration and retiring baby boomers." — the statement's "kept pace
    with the workforce" rendered with the mechanism spelled out.
Own verdict:
  "the labor market continues to get good grades. The unemployment rate
   in August came in at 4.1 percent, extending the longest streak in
   recorded history of U.S. unemployment at or below 4.5 percent.
   Initial unemployment claims are testing historic lows. Layoffs remain
   muted. And job gains — which gave a scare in July — picked back up in
   August, surpassing 160,000."
  → "Good grades." On Aug 13 the same rate was "a different, delicate
    balance" of two slowdowns, the −23K July payroll was foregrounded,
    and the CFO hiring-pause data was the qualifier. Here the July print
    is "a scare" that has passed and the August +160K is the fact.
Own qualifier / addition (the supply-offset argument, retained but
  demoted):
  "workforce growth has slowed as well, due to reduced immigration and
   retiring baby boomers" — one sentence, in the growth section, as an
   explanation for why weak hiring has not lifted unemployment. The
   Aug 13 warning that the offset might not be durable is not repeated.
Shift vs. prior speech (Aug 13):  HAWKISH LEAN — the Aug 13 dovish read
  ("softer under the headline") is withdrawn; labor is now "good grades"
  and used as the clean premise for hiking.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Aug 13):      same document — HAWKISH LEAN.

INFLATION
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Inflation remains elevated." [Sep 16 stmt — attribution clause
   DELETED]
  → Speaker version: he explains the deletion in his own words: "It is
    tempting to try to blame high inflation on a handful of categories
    with particularly high exposure to the Middle East conflict or to
    tariffs. But more than 60 percent of the PCE index is rising faster
    than 3 percent year over year." (footnote: "Based on a decomposition
    of PCE into 180 categories.")
  → This is the BREADTH test — Warsh's Aug 28 measure (54% of 199
    components above 3% over 12 months) — reproduced by a regional
    president with his own decomposition and a higher number. Second
    official to use it; second number (Warsh 54% / Barkin "more than 60
    percent"); the opening statement on Sept 16 carried it without a
    number ("Too many categories"). Language-cycle stage moves from
    committee-level-by-attribution to EMERGING across officials.
Own language — KEY (the verdict):
  "Today, inflation is our troublemaker. It's been above the Fed's 2
   percent target for more than five years. In July, headline PCE
   inflation was 3.7 percent. Core PCE came in at 3.3 percent."
  "The risks to inflation outweigh the risks to maximum employment.
   That's why we raised rates."
  "With inflation more than a percentage point above target, that's a
   problem."
Own language — the "passing shocks" argument, rejected:
  "There was an argument that inflation would return to target on its
   own, without any additional help from the Fed ... One problem with
   that argument, of course, is that the 'passing' shocks aren't proving
   to be short-lived, or one-off events. New tariffs are still cropping
   up. The conflict in the Middle East is ongoing. And the AI build-out
   continues to stress those supply chains."
  → On Aug 13 he steelmanned exactly this case ("Much of today's elevated
    inflation level has come from shocks, which should pass") as one of
    two open possibilities. Here it is named as the argument that lost.
    Same conclusion as Goolsbee's Sept 21 persistence presumption,
    reached from business intelligence rather than theory.
Own language — expectations, with district evidence:
  "there is a risk that current elevated levels of inflation could
   affect future inflation. I hear this in my district."
  "In the Richmond Fed's monthly surveys, growth in prices received has
   averaged 3.5 percent since late 2023. That's nearly double the average
   in the two years prior to the pandemic. In the second quarter of The
   CFO Survey ... expectations for 2027 year-over-year price growth came
   in at 4.1 percent. That is more than double the 2019 average ...
   generally 'double' is not a word I want to be using when it comes to
   price growth."
  → New numbers. Aug 13 had "Market-based measures of inflation
    expectations remain stable" on the dovish side of the ledger; Sept 22
    puts firm-level expectations on the hawkish side with data.
Own language — PASS-THROUGH (the analyst-flagged reversal, reversed
  again):
  "The firms I talked to then [2018–19] were convinced they had no
   ability to pass on costs. I hear a different story today."
  "First, there are more costs to pass on ... from tariffs and oil
   prices to be sure, but also from AI build-out spillovers, health care,
   transportation, or commodity prices."
  "businesses rediscovered the pricing lever. So, today, many are willing
   to test if they can't use it again. When they try, they are finding
   less resistance than they did prepandemic, especially from the wealthy
   and from business customers ... Lower-income consumers have become
   quite price sensitive, to be sure, but I'm increasingly finding firms
   willing to take their chances."
  "The net of all this is more inflationary pressure."
  → Aug 13 reported "Many business-to-consumer firms tell us they have
    little ability to pass on costs" — flagged then as a dovish reversal
    of May 21. Sept 22 reverses it back: firms are testing the lever and
    finding "less resistance." He reconciles the two: B2C price
    sensitivity is real at the low end, but the wealthy and business
    customers absorb increases and firms are willing to try. The
    on-the-ground pricing signal is back on the hawkish side.
Shift vs. prior speech (Aug 13):  HAWKISH — the "already on a path down"
  branch is dropped, the breadth test replaces the attribution framing,
  firm expectations are quantified on the hawkish side, and the
  pass-through read flips back.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Aug 13):      same document — HAWKISH.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
MONETARY POLICY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "raise the target range for the federal funds rate by 1/4 percentage
   point" ... "The Committee will deliver price stability." [Sep 16 stmt]
  → Speaker version: "At our meeting last week, the Federal Open Market
    Committee (FOMC) raised the federal funds rate by 25 basis points.
    This was our first move since cutting rates last December, and our
    first hike since mid-2023." ... "We are committed to returning
    inflation sustainably to our 2 percent target. Last week's hike will
    help." — "we"; a non-voter owning the decision.
Own language — why now:
  "What's changed? My answer: not that much. But that is precisely why
   it was appropriate for the FOMC to adjust our stance."
  → The most useful line for reading the Committee's reaction function.
    The hike is explained not by new information but by the ABSENCE of
    the improvement the hold had been waiting for. Same logic as Warsh
    Sept 16 ("very little information since that would make me reverse
    that decision") and Goolsbee Sept 21 (the peak-date slippage). Three
    officials, one rationale: the wait expired.
Own language — what next (open, two-sided):
  "Will additional hikes be required, and how many? We'll see."
  Downside case: "inflation could come back down in short order. Some of
   these recent shocks could reverse. Consumers could start to reach
   their limit. The investment boom could slow. Markets could correct.
   Employment could falter, making the labor market the problem child."
  Upside case: "inflation could prove more stubborn. Temporary shocks
   could drag on. New cost pressures could develop. Firming demand
   conditions could flow through to prices, as could the impact of
   today's inflation. I remember my dad saying, 'Do I need to repeat
   myself?' Like in child rearing, one 'talking-to' might not be enough."
  → Scenario framing is DESCRIPTIVE (two risk lists, no named scenarios,
    no policy response pre-committed to either). But the asymmetry is in
    the metaphor: "one talking-to might not be enough" is the only
    policy-directional image in the speech, and it points to a second
    hike. The Aug 13 open question ("Will the Fed need to raise rates
    further, or is inflation already on a path down?") has been answered
    on the first half and re-posed as "how many."
Stance characterization: NONE. Neither "restrictive" nor "accommodation"
  appears. The Aug 13 hedge ("many think [rates are] still restrictive
  enough") is gone with the dovish case it belonged to.
Cut signal status:  ABSENT.
Hike signal status: PRESENT — the September hike endorsed in the first
  person; further hikes an open, leaning question ("how many," "one
  talking-to might not be enough").
Shift vs. prior speech (Aug 13):  HAWKISH — from hike-as-open-question to
  hike-delivered-and-defended, with "how many" as the new question.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Aug 13):      same document — HAWKISH.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SUMMARY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Net signal:    HAWKISH
               Barkin's first post-hike speech is a defense of the hike
               built on the same three pillars as the Chair's press
               conference: full-employment labor market ("good
               grades"), breadth of inflation ("more than 60 percent of
               the PCE index is rising faster than 3 percent"), and the
               expiry of the wait ("What's changed? ... not that much.
               But that is precisely why"). Every dovish element of
               Aug 13 is withdrawn — the softer-under-the-headline labor
               read, the "already on a path" branch, the B2C
               can't-pass-through signal, and the "many think ... still
               restrictive enough" hedge. His proprietary intelligence
               now points the hawkish way on both pricing power and firm
               expectations. He stops short of a path ("We'll see") but
               the one directional image he offers is a second
               "talking-to."

Language watch:
  • "more than 60 percent of the PCE index is rising faster than 3
    percent" — the BREADTH TEST, second official after Warsh (54% / 199
    components; Barkin 180 categories). Stage: EMERGING. If Paulson or
    Jefferson use a share-above-3% figure, it is committee-wide.
  • "The risks to inflation outweigh the risks to maximum employment.
    That's why we raised rates." — the cleanest one-line rationale for
    the hike on record; candidate for pickup.
  • "one 'talking-to' might not be enough" — personal; the second-hike
    image.
  • "the 'passing' shocks aren't proving to be short-lived" — the
    attribution framework rejected in Barkin's words; pairs with
    Goolsbee's "persistent ones" (Sept 21). Stage: EMERGING (two
    regionals, one day apart).
  • "'double' is not a word I want to be using" — firm expectations
    entering his vocabulary as a hawkish input.
  • "firming" — direction added to the statement's "solid."

Analyst flag:  YES
  → (1) The pass-through signal has now flipped three times (May hawkish
        → Aug dovish → Sept hawkish). Treat the level of this indicator
        with care; the direction of the latest flip is consistent with
        every other input in the speech.
  → (2) Coverage gap: his Aug 31 Bloomberg Odd Lots appearance (31 min,
        "on the Surprisingly Resilient Real Economy") was never
        collected; the Aug 13 baseline may understate how far he had
        already moved before blackout.
  → (3) He gives no stance label at all. Third official (after Warsh and
        Goolsbee) to describe the post-hike setting without
        "restrictive." Watch whether the "mildly restrictive" bloc
        (Paulson, Collins, Daly, Waller) is now the outlier.
