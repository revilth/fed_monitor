GROWTH
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Own language:
  "Surprisingly resilient output further reinforces that view. GDP growth in
  2025 came in at 2.0 percent, and FOMC participants now forecast that 2026
  will come in at 2.2 percent. Both readings exceed last year's forecasts by
  about 1/2 percentage point. Labor productivity is booming, having grown
  about 2-1/2 percent per year over the past two years. The data center
  buildout has added some heat to the economy."
  → Growth is treated as upside surprise that reduces employment-mandate risk
    ("these developments point in the direction of less risk to the
    employment mandate") — i.e., growth strength is deployed as an argument
    on the hawkish side of her risk balance, not as a neutral recitation.

Shift vs. prior speech (May 27):  unchanged assessment (robust growth, AI
  productivity), but the framing now explicitly converts growth resilience
  into reduced employment risk — supporting her risk-balance shift.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (May 27):      same comparison.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
LABOR MARKET
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "the unemployment rate has changed little" [Jun 17 stmt]
  → Cook: "The latest jobs report showed that the unemployment rate was 4.2
    percent in June. That rate is roughly in line with the readings seen over
    the past year and consistent with what many economists believe is the
    natural rate of unemployment."

Own verdict:
  "The mostly steady unemployment rate suggests that the labor market has
   been stable. In fact, nearly all indicators point to stability. Claims for
   unemployment benefits have remained low, payrolls have been growing
   moderately, and job openings have picked up in the past few months."

Own language — KEY (employment risk explicitly downgraded):
  "So far, the most dire predictions about an AI job transition have not come
   to fruition. While I still see this as a significant risk, I do not see it
   as a greater risk than a year ago. In fact, I see few reasons that today's
   labor market has more risk than a year earlier. Therefore, risks on the
   employment side have diminished. The balance of risks has teetered toward
   the inflation mandate."
  → Direct reversal of her May 27 qualifier, "I view the downside risks to
    the labor market as being elevated." The seesaw metaphor (used in this
    speech to frame the 2025 decision) is resolved in the present: employment
    risks diminished, inflation side "now tilting toward the ground."

Retained caveat (subsection, not the overall verdict):
  "the low-hire, low-fire environment is hitting some groups — such as new
   entrants — particularly hard, and it may be damping worker sentiment and
   for good reason."
  → She bounds it: international and state-level evidence suggests a low-hire
    environment reflecting low population growth "does not mean that the
    labor market is likely to shift into a downturn."

Shift vs. prior speech (May 27):  HAWKISH — downside labor risk moves from
  "elevated" to "few reasons … more risk than a year earlier"; AI displacement
  risk explicitly marked as not greater than a year ago (May 27 treated it as
  a named forward monitoring item).
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (May 27):      same comparison.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
INFLATION
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Inflation remains elevated relative to the Committee's 2 percent goal"
  [Jun 17 stmt]
  → Cook: "Persistently elevated inflation imposes an unacceptable burden on
    American families, and it is the Federal Reserve's responsibility to
    restore price stability."

Benign data acknowledged — and dismissed at the level:
  "Even though this week's consumer price index and producer price index
   reports were softer than expected, they still imply that the price index
   we target rose 3.7 percent in the 12 months through June. That is 1.7
   percentage points above our 2 percent target. We have not reached our
   2 percent target in more than five years."
  → She engages the same-week CPI/PPI directly in her opening paragraphs and
    rules them insufficient: softer prints do not change the level verdict.
    An acknowledge-and-dismiss treatment — the benign prints are absorbed
    into the hawkish frame rather than conceded to.

Own language — KEY:
  "The initial assessment is easy: inflation is simply too high. The current
   rate of annual inflation is near the highest since 2023."
  → A level verdict, firmer in register than May 27's directional "clearly
    moving in the wrong direction."

Core goods escalation:
  "Core inflation is also coming in well above what I previously anticipated,
   driven by core goods prices, which have been increasing at a striking 5
   percent annual pace so far this year. … Rising core goods prices underscore
   the fact that the recent acceleration in inflation is not only an energy
   price story."
  → Analytical move against the energy-shock explanation: breadth, not just
    the Middle East, is driving the overshoot.

Two named reasons risks stay "strongly weighted toward higher inflation":
  1. "the AI buildout does not show signs of slowing. To date, companies have
     announced more than $1.5 trillion in data center plans, only a small
     portion of which has been realized" — plus robotics capex that "may
     expand sizably in the coming years." (Carries forward her May 27
     third-vector framing.)
  2. Persistence risk from shock-stacking on a five-year overshoot: "these
     shocks come as inflation has been elevated relative to our target for
     five years. Firms' pricing and wage decisions may depend more on what
     inflation has been rather than its source — implying a risk that the high
     inflation we have seen boosts inflation going forward."

Expectations — comfort explicitly bounded:
  "Anchored inflation expectations comfort me only to the extent that they
   tell me that people believe we will do what is necessary to get inflation
   to target — they do not tell us what those policy actions need to be. This
   sign of public confidence in the Fed is reassuring, but it does not mean
   that we can take our eye off the ball."
  → Converts the committee's standard reassurance leg (anchored expectations)
    into an obligation-to-act argument. Wages: "most measures of wages so far
    have continued to decelerate and appear consistent with 2 percent in the
    recent readings."

Shift vs. prior speech (May 27):  HAWKISH — level verdict ("simply too high,"
  near-highest since 2023) on top of May 27's directional alarm; core-goods
  breadth argument added; benign June data explicitly ruled insufficient;
  anchored-expectations comfort bounded.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (May 27):      same comparison.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
MONETARY POLICY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  June 17 hold endorsed as her own vote: "I voted with the rest of the FOMC
  last month to keep rates steady. I supported this stance, because the two
  main factors that have pushed up inflation over the past year — tariffs and
  the conflict in the Middle East — should, in theory, result in only
  short-lived increases in inflation."

Own language:
  "At this juncture, I see it as prudent to give a bit more time to observe
   how inflation unfolds from here."

Own language — KEY (conditional hike signal maintained):
  "If we do not see signs of disinflation soon, I am prepared to act. I am
   fully committed to reaching our inflation target, and this commitment is
   unwavering."
  → Maintains her May 27 conditional ("I am prepared to raise rates, if the
    expected disinflation does not appear in a timely manner"), with the
    trigger tightened from "in a timely manner" to "soon" and the action word
    generalized to "act" — in context (an inflation-risk passage with no
    labor offset), the direction is tightening. Framing is descriptive/
    conditional, not a named scenario with a specified trigger.

Cut backstop ABSENT:
  May 27 carried an explicit labor backstop ("would be prepared to adjust my
  policy stance downward should the labor market deteriorate"). No equivalent
  appears anywhere in the July 15 text — consistent with her explicit
  downgrade of employment risk. The conditionality now runs one way.

Q&A (wire-reported; no official transcript):
  Policy rate characterized as "mildly restrictive"; "The FOMC can take its
  time, I can take my time, to observe more data to understand whether it's
  really restrictive or not."
  → A restrictiveness-uncertainty framing: if the stance is only mildly
    restrictive — and possibly not restrictive — the bar for further
    tightening is lower. [Inference: this supplies the analytical bridge
    between "hold today" and "prepared to act"; she does not state it as
    such.]

Closing:
  "While I will decline to predict the path of policy today, I will
   underscore that I am committed to returning inflation to our 2 percent
   goal." Also: "I look forward to engaging with my fellow Committee members
   at our next meeting in two weeks."

Cut signal status: ABSENT — the May 27 labor backstop is dropped.
Hike signal status: PRESENT — conditional on no "signs of disinflation soon."

Shift vs. prior speech (May 27):  HAWKISH — hike conditional maintained with
  a tighter trigger word; cut backstop dropped; restrictiveness of the current
  stance questioned (Q&A).
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (May 27):      same comparison.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SUMMARY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Net signal:    HAWKISH. Cook's post-June-17 outlook debut keeps the
               conditional hike signal, drops the employment backstop,
               explicitly downgrades labor risk versus a year ago, bounds the
               comfort from anchored expectations, and rules the benign June
               CPI/PPI insufficient at the level ("still … 3.7 percent").
               Her conditional is prospective — set on the absence of
               disinflation "soon," not on any single print — so the soft
               June data do not touch it. With Waller's July 13 data trigger
               technically unmet, Cook's "prepared to act" is the clearest
               standing hike conditional carried by a voter into the blackout.

               She is more hawkish than Williams (same day: "peaked," patient
               hold) and than Goolsbee (Jul 14: conditional normalization),
               and aligned in vigilance with Warsh's "not mission
               accomplished" — but with an explicit rate conditional that
               Warsh deliberately withholds.

Language watch:
  • "inflation is simply too high" — Cook's level verdict; compare Williams's
    same-day "unquestionably too high" — the two formulations converge on a
    shared "too high" register. Stage: EMERGING (two speakers, same day).
  • "I am prepared to act" — hike conditional; Cook and Waller both carry
    one. Stage: EMERGING (two Just Voters).
  • "mildly restrictive" (Q&A) — restrictiveness-uncertainty framing; if it
    migrates into other officials' language, the bar for tightening drops.
    Stage: DISPERSED.
  • Anchored expectations "do not tell us what those policy actions need to
    be" — inverts the standard reassurance leg into an action argument;
    watch for pickup. Stage: DISPERSED.

Analyst flag:  YES
  → (1) Cook acknowledges the benign CPI/PPI and dismisses them at the level.
    Her trigger is disinflation "soon." If July and August prints extend the
    June pattern, does she count that as "signs of disinflation" — or does
    the 3.7% level keep the conditional armed regardless? The September
    meeting hinges on which reading she applies.
  → (2) Tension to assess: "mildly restrictive" (possibly not restrictive)
    plus "prepared to act" implies she doubts the current stance is
    sufficient to deliver disinflation — while her vote endorsed it as
    prudent. Watch whether "mildly restrictive" appears in her next prepared
    text (rather than Q&A), which would formalize the framing.
