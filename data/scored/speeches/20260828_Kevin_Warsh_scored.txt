GROWTH
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Fuller treatment than usual: he leads the economic assessment with growth and
uses it to argue that policy is not restraining the economy.

FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Economic activity is expanding at a solid pace ... Productivity growth and
   capital investment are strong." [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Warsh: "I am impressed by the overall performance of the economy, which
    appears to have strengthened."
  → "Strengthened" goes one step beyond the statement's "solid."
  Minutes echoed: "You may have read in the July minutes the unanimous view of
   the FOMC: Labor markets were stable, and output was solid." (Minutes text:
   "Participants generally observed that economic activity had continued to
   expand at a solid pace and that labor market conditions appeared stable.")

Own language:
  "One indicator of strength is how well an economy holds up to shocks. On that
   score, both Main Street and Wall Street have been remarkably resilient."
  Capex: "The four-quarter change in investment in equipment and intangibles has
   been around 9 percent, its highest growth rate since 2021. More than half of
   the cap-ex growth this year can likely be ascribed to the buildout related
   to AI."
  Profits: "For firms in the S&P 500, profits have grown by more than 20 percent
   over the past year. Profit margins are quite elevated, relative to history."
  Demand: "Real consumer spending has been healthy despite the shocks,
   increasing more than 2 percent over the past four quarters." PDFP "has
   increased at a pace of nearly 3 percent so far this calendar year ... the
   trend here too is positive."
  Credit: "Credit spreads on corporate bonds and leveraged loans are near the
   low ends of their historical ranges ... Credit and loan markets are showing
   few signs of policy restraint."
  Sectoral strain acknowledged and bounded: "Certain sectors—like housing and
   agriculture—are showing strains. But, on balance, I would be hard pressed
   to describe broad financial conditions as restrictive." (Scored as a stance
   judgment under MONETARY POLICY.)

Own qualifier — the one forward-looking hedge:
  "Expectations for growth in both cap-ex and corporate earnings are running
   quite high. I will continue to watch the change in their growth rates, the
   second derivative."
  → He is watching for the AI-capex impulse to decelerate, not only its level.
    This is the only passage in the speech in which the economy disappointing
    is entertained.

Shift vs. prior speech (Jul 29):  HAWKISH LEAN — July 29 called business
  investment "the most striking feature of the economy" (high-tech equipment
  and software "nearly 20 percent" four-quarter). August 28 says the economy
  "appears to have strengthened" and adds profits, margins, PDFP, consumption
  and credit conditions to the case.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Jul 14–15):   HAWKISH LEAN — testimony had "expanding at a
  solid pace, showing resilience" and "the capex surge is remarkable," with
  housing "continues to lag." Housing strain is retained here but bracketed by
  the not-restrictive judgment.

LABOR MARKET
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Job gains have kept pace with the workforce, and the unemployment rate has
   changed little." [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Warsh: "Labor markets are quite stable. The jobless rate, at 4.1 percent,
    remains low by historical standards and has not changed much for a couple
    of years. Unemployment claims, on a four-week average—an empirically
    robust real-time indicator—are near their lowest level in decades."

Own verdict:
  "In general, though, people who want to work, by and large, are holding or
   finding jobs. They may well be concerned about possible future labor
   disruptions, but as of now, I believe the labor markets are consistent with
   full employment."
  → A completion claim on the employment mandate. Stronger than any prior
    Warsh formulation (see shifts below).

Own qualifier / addition:
  "In my view, the relatively low turnover in today's labor market is partly a
   result of the significant rematching between employers and employees that
   happened at scale in the post-pandemic environment."
  "When labor supply is barely growing, monthly job gains are naturally going
   to run low. There are always areas of concern in the labor market—for
   example, among recent graduates."
  → A structural explanation for low monthly payroll gains. The July payroll
    print (−23K) is not mentioned anywhere in the speech; the sentence supplies
    a reason not to read low job gains as weakness. Consequence: a weak August
    payroll print (due Sep 4) has been pre-discounted by the Chair.

Shift vs. prior speech (Jul 29):  HAWKISH LEAN — July 29 opening statement
  used the statement sentence verbatim; the Q&A said "we're doing pretty well,
  collectively, as a country ... on the full-employment side." "Consistent
  with full employment" is firmer, and the labor-supply explanation for low
  payrolls does not appear in the July 29 transcript.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Jul 14–15):   HAWKISH LEAN — testimony: "broadly stable,"
  "in pretty good balance," "the labor market part of our mandate looks pretty
  good." Same direction, now stated as an achieved condition.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
INFLATION
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Inflation remains elevated relative to the Committee's 2 percent goal, in
   part reflecting supply shocks that have driven price increases in certain
   sectors, including energy. The Committee will deliver price stability."
   [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Warsh: "None of these measures are perfect, but they all tell a similar
    story: Inflation is running above our 2 percent target. So the Fed's
    predominant focus right now should be on prices."
  → The statement's supply-shock attribution is NOT used. The only shock
    references are "possible developments in supply chains, investment flows,
    and geopolitics" as reasons the Committee awaited data, and "The recent
    rise in overall commodity prices also bears watching." He also drops the
    minutes' framing ("in part reflecting price increases associated with
    supply shocks in certain sectors") from his summary of the minutes.
  → "The Committee will deliver price stability" is echoed as "It is the Fed's
    job to deliver stable prices" and "market prices show confidence that we
    will deliver price stability. And I can assure you . . . they're right."

Levels, stated without editorial:
  "the 12-month change in the PCE price index, stands at 3.7 percent, while the
   six-month change is 4.1 percent. The comparable measures from the consumer
   price index (CPI) are also elevated, as are the core measures of both PCE
   and CPI inflation."
  → The six-month rate above the twelve-month rate is the arithmetic of his
    case: recent momentum is not better than the trailing year.

Own language — KEY (the disclosed measure of underlying inflation):
  "The job for policymakers is to capture underlying trend inflation—that is,
   the generalized change in prices in the economy, unaffected by idiosyncratic
   factors. We want to gauge whether underlying inflation is rising, falling,
   or stuck in place. We also want to understand not just the direction of
   travel, but also the speed."
  "To try to gauge underlying inflation, I find it instructive to disaggregate
   the 199 individual components of the PCE price measure. Over the past 12
   months, 54 percent of goods and services in the PCE basket showed price
   increases above 3 percent. This is well below the post-pandemic highs of
   about 77 percent, but it remains well above the level of 32 percent in the
   two decades that preceded the pandemic."
  "Looking over just the past six months, the conclusion is similar: ... 49
   percent showed annualized price increases above 3 percent."
  → On July 29 he keyed his reaction function to "underlying inflation" and
    declined to name the measure ("without sort of fully revealing my cards
    ... my lens is broader than that"). This is the measure: a BREADTH
    statistic (share of the basket above 3%), not a level or a trimmed mean.
    A breadth measure moves slowly; a single soft monthly print barely changes
    it.

Own language — the verdict on recent data:
  "Each of these broad inflation measures has fallen significantly from their
   2022 heights. But progress over the past two years has been modest."
  "And while this summer's PCE and CPI readings were better than expected,
   they do not tell me that underlying trends have meaningfully improved."
  → Two benign prints since July 29 (July CPI, July PCE) are explicitly
    discounted.

Own language — the standard:
  "Here is my standard: We must be confident that underlying inflation is
   moving to our objective, clearly and at sufficient speed. Otherwise, we have
   work to do. That's our job . . . our mandate . . . and our charge to keep."
  → Read with the verdict above, the standard is stated and reported unmet in
    the same section.

Own language — the target and the measure (principle 3):
  "The Fed's price-stability objective of 2 percent, as measured by the
   personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index, is a firm, fixed
   target ... Price stability is not self-executing, nor is inflation
   necessarily mean-reverting. It is the Fed's job to deliver stable prices."
  → PCE is named as the target measure; the 199-component breadth statistic is
    the diagnostic. This clarifies, rather than reverses, July 29's "that's our
    number—we're sticking with it" plus "my lens is broader." "Nor is inflation
    necessarily mean-reverting" is a stated rejection of the assumption that
    shocks wash out on their own — the premise of the look-through case.

Other:
  Duration: "The responsibility for 65 months of sustained, elevated inflation
   sits squarely with the central bank. And that is where it belongs."
   (June 17 and July 29: "63 months.")
  Wages: "The data also show moderate wage growth. But in tracking underlying
   inflation, wage growth has not proven a reliable indicator of future
   inflation for a very long time." (Two footnoted Fed research papers.)
  Expectations: "measures of inflation expectations in the medium term, by and
   large, look stable ... The thing about market measures of inflation
   expectations in economic history is that they tend to look strong and
   durable until they don't ... right now they are well anchored. But they must
   be closely minded."
   → Anchored expectations are cited and then denied their usual reassurance
     value. Same structure as Daly (Aug 6) and Cook (Jul 15).

Shift vs. prior speech (Jul 29):  HAWKISH LEAN — July 29 discounted one print
  ("cannot be cured in nine weeks—or by a single month of modest price
  decreases") and gave a reaction function with an undisclosed input. August
  28 discloses the input, states a standard, and reports it unmet after two
  further benign prints. Duration clock 63 → 65 months.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Jul 14–15):   HAWKISH LEAN — testimony treated favorable
  prints as "imperfect measures of the state of underlying inflation" and said
  "we need new measures." He now supplies his own measure and a verdict.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
MONETARY POLICY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  The statement carries no forward guidance and no stance characterization;
  nothing to echo. He instead characterizes the July minutes:
  "A good majority of my colleagues and I thought the wiser course was to
   await new information in the intermeeting period ... before deciding
   whether a change in interest rate policy was advisable. And we expressed
   our joint readiness to act as circumstances might require."
  → Against the minutes text: "most participants supported maintaining the
    current target range" — "a good majority" is a fair rendering. Not
    referenced: "Several participants favored an increase of 25 basis points
    in the target range at this meeting" and "Many participants assessed that
    policy tightening would likely be necessary if inflation did not decline."
    "Readiness to act" carries no direction.

Own language — financial conditions (a stance judgment):
  "Credit and loan markets are showing few signs of policy restraint."
  "on balance, I would be hard pressed to describe broad financial conditions
   as restrictive."
  → On July 29 the claim was about the CHANGE in conditions: "we've seen a
    material tightening, not just in nominal rates, but in real rates, too."
    This is a claim about the LEVEL. Both can be true; what changes is what
    the claim licenses. The July hold was explained partly by the intermeeting
    tightening (minutes: "Various participants suggested that financial
    conditions had tightened over the intermeeting period"). A Chair who says
    the level is not restrictive is no longer using that argument. The
    minutes' "Some participants commented that financial conditions might not
    currently be sufficiently restrictive" now has the Chair's own words
    alongside it.

Own language — the seven principles (new as a stated framework):
  1. "we must interrogate reality to make sure we are not setting
     forward-looking policy based on stale or inaccurate data. Nor should we
     rely on isolated data points. Trends matter most."
     → The stated basis for discounting the summer prints.
  2. "all we observe directly is activity. We never see, and can only infer,
     what's really happening on the supply side. Hence, evaluating the current
     and expected balance between aggregate supply and demand is imprecise."
     → Carried from July 29 Q&A ("we're inferring aggregate supply"). A
       standing argument for humility about any single metric, including his
       own breadth measure.
  3. Target = 2% PCE; inflation not "necessarily mean-reverting" (see INFLATION).
  4. "Achieving both sides of our mandate over the medium term is not an
     either/or proposition ... high inflation itself is very harmful to
     economic prosperity."
     → Carry-over from July 29 Q&A ("I do not believe that price stability and
       full employment is an either-or proposition"). Not new.
  5. "short-term interest rates are the predominant tool to achieve the dual
     mandate. Unconventional policies to spur economic activity may suit
     genuine crises but should otherwise be used sparingly, if at all."
     → Narrows the balance-sheet route. On July 15 he described "two material
       instruments, interest rates and the balance sheet" in the context of
       inflation risks; on July 29 the fourth meeting question was "how much
       accommodation are we getting from the balance sheet?" Principle 5 puts
       the rate first. [Inference: the balance sheet is not the September
       instrument; the minutes also record only that the task force's findings
       "would be a useful input" for "future FOMC meetings."]
  6. "money matters ... We should pay attention to money created by the central
     bank and money that comes from the banking and financial systems."
     → Substance carried from the July 15 Senate exchange on monetary
       aggregates ("monetary policy has something to do with money"), now
       stated as a principle. Not a near-term signal.
  7. "a quieter Fed, more purposeful in its communications, is better able to
     meet its objectives ... 'At the moment of truth, there are either reasons
     or results.'"

Own language — forward guidance and the reaction function:
  "the practice has overstayed its welcome." "In normal times, the role of
   forward guidance should be limited and circumscribed. Otherwise it risks
   creating ambiguity in the name of clarity."
  "when policymakers make quasi-commitments on interest rates through the
   cycle, we inhibit our own freedom to make the right calls when it's time to
   decide."
  Hall of mirrors: "If markets rely materially on the Fed's guidance and the
   Fed relies on market prices, we are all more likely to be blinded to new
   developments."
  On an explicit reaction function: "I wish our understanding of the economy
   were so precise as to provide a mechanical, tried-and-true answer—that some
   simple function like a Taylor rule could be rigorously relied upon. But our
   knowledge just doesn't extend that far ... Providing forecasts to
   illustrate the Fed's reaction function works better in theory than in
   practice, better in the lab than in the field."
  → Tension with July 29 Q&A, where he volunteered "when he or she sees
    underlying inflation moving higher—he or she is more inclined to tighten
    policy ... That's my reaction function." What is rejected here is a
    mechanical rule with a published path; the directional disposition given
    on July 29 is not withdrawn. [Inference: the two are reconcilable as
    rule-versus-disposition, but he does not draw the distinction himself.]
  Distributional argument (new): "The most serious harm is likely to befall
   those without financial assets ... Hard-working Americans are the ones left
   to deal with inflation that is too high or jobs that suddenly appear less
   secure."
  Framing devices: "just don't call it forward guidance"; "I stand here today
   committed to a discipline, not to a decision."

AI — removed from the near-term frame:
  Extended discussion (token sales "more than $100 billion—an increase of
  500-plus percent from a year ago"; productivity, labor, market-structure
  questions), then: task-force recommendations "will come later and have no
  bearing on decisions we make in the current policy conjuncture."
  → On July 15 he argued an AI-driven one-time price change is "not
    necessarily inflationary"; on July 29 he asked whether chip-price
    increases are noticed "just because they are under the bright
    streetlight." Here AI is set aside from current policy entirely rather
    than adjudicated.

Cut signal status:  ABSENT.
Hike signal status: ABSENT as a signal. "Otherwise, we have work to do" is the
  nearest formulation — undirected as to instrument and timing. No reference
  to September, to the SEP, or to the three dissents.

Shift vs. prior speech (Jul 29):  HAWKISH LEAN — new: a published standard
  reported unmet; the not-restrictive level judgment; rates named the
  predominant tool. Carried: no-guidance regime, "not either/or" dual mandate.
  July 29's "where necessary and appropriate, we will not hesitate to act"
  becomes "Otherwise, we have work to do."
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Jul 14–15):   HAWKISH LEAN — testimony kept "leave rates
  alone / raise / cut" all open and named the balance sheet as a second
  instrument. August 28 still names no decision, but publishes a standard he
  says is not met and subordinates the balance sheet to the rate.

SUMMARY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Net signal:    HAWKISH LEAN
               The economic assessment removes the usual grounds for patience
               one by one: growth "strengthened"; labor "consistent with full
               employment," with low payroll gains pre-explained by labor
               supply; financial conditions not restrictive; wages not a
               reliable disinflation signal; anchored expectations "until
               they don't"; the summer prints "do not tell me that underlying
               trends have meaningfully improved." He then publishes the test
               he will apply — underlying inflation "moving to our objective,
               clearly and at sufficient speed" — and the metric behind it
               (54% / 49% of the PCE basket above 3% vs. a 32% pre-pandemic
               norm), and reports the test unmet. What he withholds is the
               decision: no instrument, no timing, no September reference,
               "committed to a discipline, not to a decision."

               Position relative to the Committee: on the diagnosis
               (restrictiveness, breadth, non-mean-reversion) his language now
               sits with the three dissenters, Schmid (Aug 27) and the
               minutes' "some participants." On the instrument he is with the
               majority — rates, and not yet. His breadth measure is the
               direct counterpart to Paulson's Aug 4 underlying estimate of
               "2.4, 2.8" (which strips tariff and energy effects); the two
               methods read the same data in opposite directions.

Language watch:
  • "clearly and at sufficient speed" — the stated standard. Stage: DISPERSED
    (first use). Barr (Sep 1) uses "moderating sufficiently"; Waller (Sep 3)
    answers the speed question directly. A shared "sufficient" test would be a
    shared bar rather than a shared worry.
  • 199-component PCE breadth (share above 3%) — Stage: DISPERSED (Chair only).
    Waller's July 13 breadth statistic (70% of core-services categories above
    3%) is the nearest prior. Watch whether it appears in the September
    minutes or SEP discussion.
  • "hard pressed to describe broad financial conditions as restrictive" —
    Stage: EMERGING (minutes "some participants"; Hammack, Kashkari, Logan
    dissent statements; Schmid Aug 27; now the Chair).
  • "nor is inflation necessarily mean-reverting" — anti-look-through
    principle. Stage: EMERGING (Kashkari's successive-shocks argument makes
    the same claim; minutes carry "many participants noted the possibility
    that inflation might be more persistently elevated").
  • "65 months" — duration clock, third consecutive document, incrementing.
    Stage: DISPERSED (Warsh; Barr and Waller use "five years" / "five and a
    half years").
  • "committed to a discipline, not to a decision" — the no-guidance
    formula; high media-pickup probability.

Analyst flag:  YES
  → (1) The standard is public and, by his own account, unmet. Whether "we
    have work to do" points to the rate in September or to continued
    observation is not resolved in the text. Note the asymmetry: every
    standard argument for patience is addressed and set aside; only the
    decision is withheld.
  → (2) Breadth is slow-moving by construction. Under this reaction function a
    soft August CPI or PCE print does little to the share of the basket above
    3%. Incoming prints carry less weight for the Chair than for officials
    using momentum (Waller) or attribution (Williams) tests.
  → (3) Reaction-function tension: an explicit reaction function is declined
    here five weeks after one was given in the July 29 Q&A. Rule-versus-
    disposition reconciles it; watch whether he is pressed on it.
  → (4) [Inference] Footnote 7 cites Engstrom (2026), "Anchored to the Dot
    Plot," on expectations adjusting too slowly away from prior SEP
    projections — inside a speech critical of forward guidance, three weeks
    before a projection meeting. He does not connect them. If the September
    SEP is restructured or caveated, this was the marker. Low confidence.
  → (5) His summary of the July minutes omits the "several participants" who
    favored a 25bp increase and the "many participants" who expected
    tightening to be necessary if inflation did not decline. Same device as
    July 29 (unity on the objective; dispersion on the path unmentioned).
