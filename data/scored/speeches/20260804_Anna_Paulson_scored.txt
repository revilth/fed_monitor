INFLATION
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed — AND DEFENDED:
  "Inflation remains elevated relative to the Committee's 2 percent goal, in
   part reflecting supply shocks that have driven price increases in certain
   sectors, including energy." [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Paulson: "you've got inflation that's too high" — verbatim carry-over of
    her May 19 "Inflation is too high," unqualified.

Own language — KEY, AND NEW TO THE CORPUS (the underlying-inflation number):
  "I'm trying to measure the depth of water in a pool and there's rocks being
   thrown into the pool — tariffs, energy shocks, things like that. And so
   what's the level of the pool? When I look at underlying inflation, when you
   try to strip out the effects of those things, I see it somewhere in the
   range of 2.4, 2.8. Maybe there was a little bit of mild progress over the
   last several months, but I want to see more progress on that and that's what
   I'm really focused on to gauge our progress."
  → The most consequential disclosure in the interview. She has never put a
    figure on underlying inflation before. Against headline PCE of 3.7 percent
    (Cook, Aug 5), her working estimate is 2.4–2.8 percent — i.e. 0.4 to 0.8
    percentage points above target, not 1.7.
  → This is the analytical foundation of the median voter's hold vote, and it
    is a materially more benign reading of the same data than the dissent bloc
    derives from it. It also gives the analyst a concrete, trackable series:
    her stated pool level is now a number that can move.

Supply-shock look-through — DEFENDED against Kashkari BY NAME:
  Liesman puts Kashkari's position to her directly ("there's a role for the Fed
  to play in addressing supply shocks").
  → Paulson: "I have definitely been of the view that you should look through
    supply shocks. I think that the June PCE inflation data kind of validate
    that. We saw there that when we had the lull in the conflict in the Middle
    East, oil prices came down, we got inflation relief. If I had tried to
    affect that through monetary policy, I would have been too late. It
    wouldn't have hit at the right time."
  → A TIMING argument, not a level argument: she does not claim the shocks are
    small, she claims policy cannot arrive in time to offset them. This is the
    first direct majority-side rebuttal of the dissent bloc's central doctrinal
    claim, and it is the exact rebuttal the July 31 Kashkari scored file
    flagged as the thing to watch for.

Bounded — second-round effects:
  "are there any second round effects? That's where monetary policy has a real
   role to play, to keep those shocks in their lane."
  → The look-through is conditional, not absolute. Her trip wire is pass-through
    into the underlying rate, consistent with her May 19 three-factor framework
    where inflation expectations were the named trip wire.

New ground-level evidence — pricing power:
  "I hear from price setters, CEOs of big companies, especially ones that are
   selling directly to consumers, that they don't feel like they can pass along
   cost increases to consumers because consumers aren't in a position to be
   able to absorb it."
  → New in the corpus and genuinely informative: a demand-side ceiling on
    pass-through, which is the mechanism that would prevent second-round
    effects. She files it under evidence for restrictiveness.
  → ⚠ INTERNAL TENSION worth pressing: in the growth section the consumer
    "looks really strong"; here consumers "aren't in a position to absorb"
    price increases. Both are used to support her hold — strength shows no
    recession risk, weakness shows no pass-through risk. She is not asked to
    reconcile them.

Shift vs. prior speech / pre-FOMC (May 19):  DOVISH LEAN on substance — Same
  "too high" verdict, but the underlying estimate of 2.4–2.8 percent is more
  benign than anything in the May 19 text, and she now cites the June PCE
  relief as positive VALIDATION of look-through rather than treating the shock
  sequence as a growing concern. May 19's "after five years of elevated
  inflation and a series of supply shocks, this is a real concern" (on
  expectations fragility) has no equivalent here.

MONETARY POLICY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  Endorses the July 29 hold, and rules out that it was marginal:
  "for me, it was not a close call."
  → Directly relevant to reading the 9–3. The median voter says her own vote
    was not near the line. The split is wider than a one-vote-from-flipping
    reading would suggest.

Own language — the stable label, UNCHANGED:
  "I think we need policy that's mildly restrictive. And I think policy has
   been mildly restrictive, to get underlying inflation back down to 2% in an
   acceptable time period."
  → "Mildly restrictive" has been her characterization since March 6 and
    through March 27 and May 19. Four consecutive uses across five months. The
    label is stable; what moved is the conditional attached to it.

Own language — KEY (the tightening option, now in HER OWN VOICE):
  "I need to see progress from here."
  "if we don't see that progress, then we have to be open to recalibrating
   monetary policy. We need to get to 2%."
  Liesman: "And that means higher rates in that sense, right?"
  → "Could be higher rates, could be same rates for longer. I'm keeping an open
    mind about what's going to be appropriate."
  → ESCALATION vs. May 19 in grammatical person, which is the signal. On May 19
    she validated OTHERS' pricing — "it is healthy that market participants have
    taken on board … scenarios where further tightening becomes necessary." On
    August 4 higher rates are an option she names for herself, conditional on
    the absence of progress. The May 19 analyst flag asked whether her
    tightening language was genuine or hedging; it has now moved from
    third-person endorsement to first-person conditional.
  → Note the option set is TWO-SIDED in the hawkish direction only: higher
    rates or the same rates for longer. Cuts are not mentioned anywhere in the
    interview.

Neutral rate — the dissenters' challenge CONCEDED as the key question, NOT
resolved:
  Liesman: "there are people who say that all of this investment raises the
  neutral rate, and so maybe you're not as restrictive as you think you are."
  → Paulson: "that's one of the things that I am assessing constantly. I think
    that's the key question, right? So I need to see progress from here."
  → She grants that whether the stance is genuinely restrictive is THE key
    question — which is precisely the dissenters' argument — and then answers
    it empirically rather than theoretically: progress in the underlying rate
    is her test of restrictiveness. Per the Type D neutral-rate precision rule:
    she does NOT establish that policy is restrictive; she states a method for
    finding out. Her three named pieces of evidence for "mildly restrictive"
    are subdued housing, wage growth consistent with 2 percent, and the
    pricing-power ceiling. Against them she weighs AI capex, the investment
    boom and robust equities.

Forward guidance — explicitly withheld:
  "I just told you I've got an open mind about where we go from here. So I

   weighing the evidence along with everybody else."

SUMMARY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Net signal:    HAWKISH LEAN on the policy option set; DOVISH on the underlying
               data read. Both directions are real and they do not cancel — they
               describe a median voter who has become more willing to name a
               hike while believing the inflation problem is smaller than the
               dissenters do.

               The hawkish move is grammatical and therefore easy to miss: on
               May 19 tightening was something markets could sensibly price; on
               August 4 it is "could be higher rates," said in the first person,
               conditional on the absence of progress. The May 19 analyst flag
               asked whether her tightening talk was genuine or hedging. It has
               hardened.

               The dovish content is the number. Stripping tariffs and energy
               out of the pool, she puts underlying inflation at 2.4–2.8
               percent. Against a 3.7 percent headline, that is a far more
               benign problem than the dissent bloc describes, and it is why her
               vote "was not a close call." She then defends the look-through
               doctrine on TIMING grounds — policy cannot arrive when the shock
               does — citing the June PCE relief as validation.

               What she did NOT do is resolve the restrictiveness question. She
               conceded it is "the key question," listed evidence on both sides,
               and reduced it to an empirical test: does the pool level fall
               from here. That makes her next two inflation prints, read through
               her 2.4–2.8 estimate, the highest-value observable for the
               September meeting.
