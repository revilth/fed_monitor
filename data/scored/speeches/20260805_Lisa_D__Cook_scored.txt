PER-SPEECH ANALYSIS

2026-08-05 | Lisa D. Cook | Governor | Just Voter
"Outlook for the U.S. and Alaskan Economies"
2026 Economic Luncheon of the Anchorage Economic Development Corporation,
  Anchorage, Alaska
SOURCE: https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/cook20260805a.htm

CLASSIFICATION: Type A — Economic Outlook, with a regional (Alaska) section
  and an embedded special-topic section on consumer sentiment. The national
  outlook and monetary policy sections are scored as Type A; the sentiment
  section is scored where it carries policy content.

REFERENCE FOMC:    July 29, 2026 (HOLD 3.50–3.75%; 9–3; Hammack, Kashkari and
                   Logan dissenting in favor of a 25bp HIKE; substantive
                   statement text frozen vs. June 17)
PRE-FOMC BASELINE: July 15, 2026  "Economic Outlook" (Exchequer Club of
                   Washington D.C.; scored at
                   data/scored/speeches/20260715_Lisa_D__Cook_scored.txt)
POST-FOMC PRIOR:   N/A — this is Cook's first communication of any kind since
                   the July 29 meeting. July 15 is therefore both the prior
                   speech and the pre-FOMC baseline; the two comparisons are
                   the same and are reported once.

SIGNIFICANCE: This is the FIRST communication from any member of the nine-vote
  July 29 majority since the meeting. Everything heard from the Committee
  between July 29 and today came from the three dissenters (July 31). Cook is
  also the majority-side voter who carried the most explicit hike conditional
  into the meeting, so her speech is the first direct read on whether the
  majority's center is moving toward the dissenting bloc.
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

GROWTH
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Economic activity is expanding at a solid pace" [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Cook: "Overall economic growth in the U.S. remains solid this year."
    Near-verbatim adoption of the statement's adjective.

  "Productivity growth and capital investment are strong" [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Cook: "Overall business investment rose at a 10 percent annual rate in
    the first half of the year."

Own language:
  "After being adjusted for inflation, output grew at a 1.8 percent pace
   through the first half of the year and is on track to grow at a faster
   pace in the second half."
  → Acceleration call. She is not forecasting demand-side disinflation from
    slowing activity — the opposite. This matters because it removes one
    channel by which inflation could fall without policy action, and it sits
    in tension with the three disinflation channels she names later (all
    three are supply- or base-effect channels, none demand-side).

  "U.S. households appear resilient, with consumer spending advancing at
   close to a 2 percent rate in the first half. Housing continued to be a
   soft spot, with the level of residential investment edging down about
   3 percent."
  → The only named weak sector. She does not develop it into a policy
    implication and does not connect it to the level of rates.

Shift vs. prior speech / pre-FOMC (Jul 15):  unchanged, marginal hawkish lean
  — Same "solid/resilient" verdict. The July 15 speech leaned on forecast
  ("FOMC participants now forecast that 2026 will come in at 2.2 percent");
  today she reports realized H1 at 1.8 percent but adds an explicit H2
  acceleration call. Net framing is unchanged; the acceleration language is a
  marginal hawkish addition. Her July 15 device of converting growth strength
  into reduced employment risk ("these developments point in the direction of
  less risk to the employment mandate") is NOT repeated.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
LABOR MARKET
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Job gains have kept pace with the workforce, and the unemployment rate has
   changed little" [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Cook: "In June, the unemployment rate was 4.2 percent. That rate has
    barely changed from a year earlier and aligns with what many economists
    believe is the natural rate of unemployment."

Own verdict (overall, stated in the summary paragraph):
  "the labor market appears to be stable, in a low-hire, low-fire
   environment."
  → Her topic-opening verdict in the labor paragraph is consistent:
    "the labor market has remained resilient over the past year."

Own quantification — NEW:
  "Job growth over the past year has been modest. However, it picked up
   during the spring months, averaging more than 100,000 jobs added per month
   in the April through June period."
  → New specificity vs. July 15's qualitative "payrolls have been growing
    moderately." Against the shrinking labor-force growth she describes
    elsewhere, 100k+/month is comfortably above breakeven. Marginal hawkish.

Carry-over — AI displacement risk (unchanged, near-verbatim):
  Aug 5: "Thus far, the most dire predictions about AI job losses have not
   materialized. I still see this development as a significant risk but one
   that has not grown over the past year."
  Jul 15: "So far, the most dire predictions about an AI job transition have
   not come to fruition. While I still see this as a significant risk, I do
   not see it as a greater risk than a year ago."
  → Recycled formulation. Neutral; no signal. Flagged here specifically so it
    is not mistaken for a new statement.

Carry-over — low-hire caveat (subsection, not the overall verdict):
  "The low-hire, low-fire equilibrium hits some groups, including new
   entrants, especially hard and may restrain worker sentiment for good
   reason." Bounded exactly as on July 15: "international and state-level
   evidence suggests that low hiring rates, when they reflect slow population
   growth, do not signal an impending downturn by themselves."

NOT REPEATED — the July 15 risk-balance sentence:
  July 15 carried "I see few reasons that today's labor market has more risk
  than a year earlier. Therefore, risks on the employment side have
  diminished." Today's speech contains no equivalent claim sourced to the
  employment side. The risk-balance conclusion still appears — but its
  location and its driver have both moved (see INFLATION).

Shift vs. prior speech / pre-FOMC (Jul 15):  unchanged — Same stable/resilient
  verdict, same AI caveat, same low-hire bounding, with added payroll
  specificity on the firm side. No deterioration acknowledged.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
INFLATION
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed — AND QUALIFIED (KEY):
  "Inflation remains elevated relative to the Committee's 2 percent goal, in
   part reflecting supply shocks that have driven price increases in certain
   sectors, including energy." [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Cook: "Elevated energy prices due to the conflict in the Middle East have
    contributed significantly to inflation over the past year, but it is not
    the only factor. Core prices, which exclude food and energy costs, rose
    3.3 percent over the same period."
  → This is the first majority-side response to the analytical question the
    July 31 dissent statements posed. Hammack and Logan attacked the
    statement's supply-shock attribution as insufficient and argued the
    residual is demand. Cook does NOT endorse the statement's attribution
    unqualified: she affirms the energy leg, then says explicitly it "is not
    the only factor" and supplies core at 3.3 percent as the evidence.
  → But she does not follow the dissenters to a demand-side conclusion. She
    names a second, specific source instead:

  "This year has brought two unexpected sources of price pressure: The Middle
   East conflict has driven the cost of energy and certain other goods
   higher, and companies are ramping up capital spending to build out
   artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure. That investment wave has
   lifted prices for semiconductors, high-tech equipment, software, and
   utilities."
  → [Inference: the AI-capex channel is a demand-driven relative price shift,
    not a supply shock in the statement's sense — so Cook has in substance
    conceded part of the dissenters' point while declining their framing and
    their policy conclusion. She does not characterize it as demand-side
    herself; this reading is not stated in the text.]

Risk balance — RELOCATED and RE-SOURCED:
  Aug 5: "Taken together, these developments have shifted the balance of
   risks toward inflation and away from the labor market."
  → Same destination as July 15 ("The balance of risks has teetered toward
    the inflation mandate"), but the driver has flipped. On July 15 the tilt
    was produced by the employment side improving; today it is produced by
    the two price-pressure sources, and the sentence sits in the inflation
    paragraph. Substantively similar conclusion, different mechanism.

Own language — KEY (level verdict, carry-over):
  "my assessment is simple: Inflation is too high. This has been my long-held
   view."
  Jul 15: "The initial assessment is easy: inflation is simply too high."
  → Recycled, register unchanged. Reinforced with duration: inflation "has
    exceeded the Federal Open Market Committee's (FOMC) 2 percent target for
    more than five years."

Data: PCE 3.7 percent 12-month through June — identical figure to July 15.
  Core PCE 3.3 percent.

SOFTENED — the core-goods breadth alarm:
  July 15 carried "Core inflation is also coming in well above what I
  previously anticipated, driven by core goods prices, which have been
  increasing at a striking 5 percent annual pace so far this year. … Rising
  core goods prices underscore the fact that the recent acceleration in
  inflation is not only an energy price story."
  Today core is reported flatly at 3.3 percent. The "striking" escalation
  language, the 5 percent core-goods figure, and the breadth argument are all
  dropped. The goods pressure is instead attributed to the AI buildout and
  forecast to ease. Dovish.

CONCEDED — June improvement:
  "The inflation picture improved modestly in June, the most recent month for
   which we have data. However, I would not put too much weight on a single
   data point, especially in what remains a highly uncertain environment."
  → On July 15 she engaged the same-week soft CPI/PPI and ruled them
    insufficient at the level ("they still imply that the price index we
    target rose 3.7 percent"). Today she grants the improvement in her own
    voice before bounding it. A real, if small, concession — and it sets up
    the changed trigger word in MONETARY POLICY.

THE STRUCTURAL INVERSION — three named disinflationary forces (NEW):
  July 15 named TWO reasons risks stay "strongly weighted toward higher
  inflation." Today she names THREE forces pushing the other way, and the
  section is the analytical centre of the speech:
   1. "the effects of tariffs announced last year on the price level are
      mostly behind us. … We should see some disinflation as the early months
      of tariff pass-through drop out of the inflation window."
   2. "while oil prices continue to be elevated relative to early this year
      because of the Middle East conflict, many forecasters suggest that they
      will come down by the end of the year, providing some deflationary
      relief."
   3. "some of the recent price pressure in goods is due to the relative
      demand shift from the AI buildout … As supply chains adjust and
      sector-specific efficiency gains accrue, I believe some of the
      inflationary pressure coming from the AI buildout will ease."
  → Item 3 is a DIRECT REVERSAL on her single most-emphasized inflation-risk
    vector. On July 15 the AI buildout was reason #1 that risks were
    "strongly weighted toward higher inflation" — "the AI buildout does not
    show signs of slowing. To date, companies have announced more than $1.5
    trillion in data center plans, only a small portion of which has been
    realized." Twenty-one days later the same vector is a source of coming
    disinflation. The $1.5 trillion figure and the "only a small portion
    realized" argument do not appear. This is the most consequential single
    change in the speech.
  → Each force is hedged ("the exact path of tariff policy remains
    uncertain"; "similar to tariff policy, uncertainty remains high"; "some
    of the inflationary pressure … will ease"). None is asserted as done.

RETAINED and SHARPENED — entrenchment risk:
  "With five years of above-target inflation, the risk grows that higher
   inflation may become entrenched in price- and wage-setting behavior,
   leading to persistence that would be much harder for us to attack. The
   longer inflation is above target, the more likely this scenario becomes.
   Thus, while we might be able to afford to wait for longer in a different
   environment, we do not have that luxury in this one."
  → Carries forward July 15's reason #2 (shock-stacking on a five-year
    overshoot). The closing clause — "we do not have that luxury in this
    one" — is NEW and is the hardest sentence in the speech. It is an
    explicit statement that time is not free, and it is the counterweight
    that keeps the disinflation-forces section from reading as a stand-down.

Shift vs. prior speech / pre-FOMC (Jul 15):  DOVISH LEAN on the risk
  architecture, level verdict unchanged — The "too high" verdict, the 3.7
  percent figure and the five-year duration argument are all recycled intact.
  What changed is the structure underneath: July 15 named two escalation
  channels and dismissed the soft data; August 5 names three disinflation
  channels, concedes June improved, drops the core-goods breadth alarm, and
  moves the AI buildout from the escalation column to the disinflation
  column. Partially offset by a sharper entrenchment warning and by her
  qualification of the statement's supply-shock attribution.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
MONETARY POLICY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  July 29 hold endorsed as her own vote, with reasons given:
  "For these three reasons, I felt it was appropriate not to change rates
   while we see how these factors evolve."
  → Note what this does: it is a majority voter supplying an explicit
    rebuttal to the three hike dissents. Her stated ground for not hiking is
    not that inflation is acceptable — she says it is too high — but that
    three disinflation channels are already in train. That is a direct answer
    to Hammack's "now is the time for the FOMC to act" and to Kashkari's
    "modest hikes now reduce the risk of more aggressive tightening later."

Own language — KEY (hike conditional, direction now NAMED):
  "As I have described, inflation is too high, and I consider the risks to
   the inflation side of the dual mandate higher than the risks to the
   employment side at this point. As such, I am prepared to act by raising
   rates, if necessary."
  → July 15 read "If we do not see signs of disinflation soon, I am prepared
    to act." The verb was unspecified and the direction had to be read from
    context. Today she names it: "by raising rates." Escalation in
    explicitness. She remains the only majority-side voter on record with a
    directional rate conditional.

NEW — cost-of-hiking caveat, and an explicit escape clause:
  "The labor market and output growth are currently stable. I would consider
   how a rate increase could negatively affect that stability. Still, I would
   support an increase, if it becomes necessary to bring inflation down. It
   may not."
  → Two additions with no July 15 equivalent. First, a stated cost to
    hiking — a partial restoration of labor-side weight that was entirely
    absent on July 15, where the conditionality "now runs one way." Second,
    "It may not." — a flat two-word concession that the hike may never be
    required. Nothing of this kind appears in the July 15 text.
  → This is NOT a restored cut backstop. Her May 27 formulation ("would be
    prepared to adjust my policy stance downward should the labor market
    deteriorate") remains dropped. The caveat is a brake on hiking, not a
    path to easing.

TRIGGER WORD CHANGED (KEY — one word):
  Jul 15: "If we do not see signs of disinflation soon, I am prepared to act."
  Aug 5:  "If I do not see signs of continued disinflation soon, I am
           prepared to act."
  → "continued" presupposes that disinflation has begun. Read together with
    her concession that June "improved modestly," the condition she now needs
    met is continuation of an already-started process rather than initiation
    of one. On that reading the bar for the conditional to stay unfired is
    lower than it was on July 15, and the July and August prints need only
    extend June's pattern.
  → The competing reading is that "continued" raises the bar by requiring a
    sustained run rather than a single soft print. The text does not settle
    it. Flagged for the analyst rather than resolved here.

Cut signal status:  ABSENT — May 27's labor backstop remains dropped.
Hike signal status: PRESENT, and more explicit than July 15 ("by raising
  rates"), but with a materially weaker condition attached ("continued
  disinflation," plus "It may not," plus a stated cost to hiking).

"Mildly restrictive" — NOT formalized:
  The July 15 scored file flagged whether her wire-reported Q&A framing of
  the stance as "mildly restrictive" would migrate into prepared text. It did
  not. This speech contains no characterization of the stance's
  restrictiveness at all — no "restrictive," no "neutral," no reference to
  the level of rates relative to either. That July 15 analyst flag resolves
  NO for now.

Shift vs. prior speech / pre-FOMC (Jul 15):  DOVISH LEAN — the conditional is
  stated more explicitly while the condition itself is made easier to
  satisfy, a cost to hiking is acknowledged for the first time, and "It may
  not" is added. She has made the threat clearer and less likely to be
  carried out in the same paragraph.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
EMBEDDED SPECIAL TOPIC — CONSUMER SENTIMENT
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Scored because she converts it into a mandate argument, not for its own sake.

  "Consumer sentiment, by many measures, is lower than one would expect in a
   solid labor market, and perceptions of job availability have continued to
   worsen. In outreach calls, I hear that vulnerable households are
   especially dissatisfied with the economy."
  → Note the one genuinely soft data point in the speech is a labor
    perception ("perceptions of job availability have continued to worsen"),
    and she files it under sentiment rather than under the labor market. It
    does not touch her labor verdict.

Three causes given: AI job-market uncertainty; decades-long structural cost
  increases (housing, education, health care, elder care, childcare); and
  "the high inflation experienced over the past five years."

Policy conversion — HAWKISH-SUPPORTING:
  "In sum, the reasons for low sentiment are real and are deeply concerning.
   They require a varied and broad policy approach, largely outside the scope
   of monetary policy. But we have our part to play. As a monetary
   policymaker, I believe that the best thing we can do in our roles is to
   ensure that inflation returns to and stays at target."
  → Soft sentiment is routinely a dovish input; Cook routes it to the
    opposite conclusion. Weak sentiment becomes an additional reason to
    prioritize the inflation leg of the mandate. Reinforced in the
    conclusion: "If you take away one thing from this talk, I hope it is that
    I am firmly committed to restoring price stability."

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
ALASKA SECTION — regional, low policy content
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Explicitly ring-fenced from policy: "When I consider monetary policy, I focus
on the national picture I just described." One line worth noting for the
inflation record — "inflation pressure appears to be picking up in the most
recent readings" (Alaska-specific, not a national claim; do not read across).
Remainder is regional description: 4.4 percent unemployment, shrinking
working-age population, oil-and-gas fiscal-vs-household asymmetry.

SUMMARY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Net signal:    DOVISH LEAN vs. her own July 15 baseline — from a starting
               point that was the most hawkish on the majority side of the
               Committee. In absolute terms she remains hawkish: inflation is
               "too high," the risk balance is tilted to inflation, the only
               directional conditional she carries is a hike, and she now
               names it as such.

               The change is in the architecture, not the verdict. Twenty-one
               days ago she named two channels by which inflation risk was
               "strongly weighted" higher; today she names three by which it
               should come down — and the AI buildout, which was her single
               most-emphasized escalation vector on July 15, has crossed to
               the disinflation side. She concedes June improved, drops the
               striking core-goods breadth argument, adds a cost-of-hiking
               caveat and the words "It may not," and softens her trigger
               from disinflation to CONTINUED disinflation. Against that she
               names the hike direction explicitly for the first time and
               sharpens the entrenchment warning to "we do not have that
               luxury in this one."

               The honest formulation: she made the conditional more explicit
               and its condition less likely to fire, in the same paragraph.

               For the committee read, the more important content is that
               this is the first majority-side voter to speak since July 29,
               and she does two things the dissenters will notice. She
               qualifies the statement's supply-shock attribution in her own
               voice — "but it is not the only factor" — which is the
               dissenters' opening argument. And she then refuses their
               conclusion, grounding the hold in three disinflation channels
               rather than in any claim that inflation is acceptable. The
               majority's answer to the 9–3 split, at least from Cook, is
               "the process is already working," not "you are wrong about the
               level."

Language watch:
  • "but it is not the only factor" (on the statement's supply-shock
    attribution) — the single most contested formulation in the cycle.
    Hammack and Logan attacked it from the demand side on July 31; Cook now
    qualifies it from the majority side. If Jefferson, Williams or Paulson
    also decline to defend the attribution unqualified, the September
    statement's inflation sentence is the language most likely to change.
    Stage: EMERGING (three officials now qualifying it, from two directions).
  • "prepared to act by raising rates" — Cook's conditional, direction now
    named. Waller carries a data-triggered version (Jul 13). Watch whether
    any third majority voter adopts an explicit rate conditional. Stage:
    EMERGING (two Just Voters).
  • "continued disinflation" — the changed trigger word. Watch whether other
    officials adopt "continued," which would signal the majority has
    collectively accepted that disinflation has begun. Stage: DISPERSED (one
    speaker).
  • AI buildout as a source of coming disinflation ("supply chains adjust and
    sector-specific efficiency gains accrue") — a reversal of the prevailing
    committee treatment of AI capex as an inflationary demand shock. If this
    spreads it materially lowers the committee's inflation-risk assessment
    without any data changing. Stage: DISPERSED (one speaker, new).
  • "we do not have that luxury in this one" (on waiting) — entrenchment
    urgency. Rhetorically close to Hammack's July 31 "the longer that high
    inflation persists, the more challenging and costly it can be to bring it
    back down." Stage: EMERGING (a majority voter and a dissenter converging
    on the persistence argument while disagreeing on the action).

Analyst flag:  YES
  → (1) PRIMARY: the AI-buildout reversal. On July 15 the buildout "does not
    show signs of slowing" with "$1.5 trillion in data center plans, only a
    small portion of which has been realized" — reason #1 that risks were
    weighted higher. On August 5 it is a source of coming disinflation. No
    data between the two dates obviously forces this; the July 29 meeting sits
    between them. Is this Cook updating on evidence, or is it the majority
    position she signed onto at the meeting appearing in her prepared text?
    The July 28–29 minutes (Aug 19) should show whether the participant
    discussion of AI capex moved the same way. This is the highest-value
    check available before Jackson Hole.
  → (2) The trigger word. "Signs of disinflation" (Jul 15) → "signs of
    CONTINUED disinflation" (Aug 5), following her concession that June
    improved modestly. Does "continued" lower her bar — she needs only
    continuation from June — or raise it by demanding a sustained run? The
    July PCE print is the first test, and her reading of it determines
    whether the most explicit hike conditional on the majority side survives
    into September.
  → (3) The disinflation channels are all supply-side or base-effect, while
    her growth call is for ACCELERATION in H2. She therefore expects
    disinflation without any demand moderation. If oil does not fall by
    year-end as she assumes ("many forecasters suggest"), two of her three
    channels are gone and only the tariff base effect remains. Worth pressing:
    what is her fallback if the oil assumption fails?
  → (4) Coverage note for the record — this speech was delivered August 5 and
    was not collected or scored on the August 6 morning run; it was recovered
    manually. See the August 5 daily report for the pipeline gap.
