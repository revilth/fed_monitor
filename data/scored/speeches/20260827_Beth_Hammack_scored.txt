CLASSIFICATION: Type A — Economic Outlook, delivered as an unscripted
  broadcast interview. Covers inflation, policy stance and neutral in depth.
  No growth or labor question was asked.

PRE-FOMC BASELINE: June 2, 2026  "It Takes Two to Make an Economy Go Right"

INFLATION
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "Inflation remains elevated relative to the Committee's 2 percent goal, in
   part reflecting supply shocks that have driven price increases in certain
   sectors, including energy." [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Hammack: "we've been in an inflationary situation for more than five
    years. It's been running well above our target." She does not echo the
    supply-shock clause. Asked directly whether the shocks "will go away," she
    answers with persistence.

Own language — the data read:
  "that PCE number that came in at 0.25 was exactly where our nowcast was
   projecting it to be ... If you annualize that number, that gets you to
   about 3% inflation per year."
  → An in-line print is read as confirmation of roughly 3 percent inflation,
    not as relief. Contrast Paulson's Aug 4 "mild progress" on underlying
    inflation.

Own language — KEY (layered shocks and persistence):
  Liesman: "Did you see something more sinister in that number yesterday?"
  "I wouldn't use that word. I mean what I would say is that worrisome,
   persistent. I think what I'm seeing is more persistence in it. We have had
   these layered shocks, and I think one of the questions is, are they going
   to neatly roll off and just disappear into the background, or are there
   going to be new shocks that are going to come on that are going to keep
   things more persistent?"
  → "Layered shocks" is her form of Kashkari's Jul 31 successive-shocks
    doctrine. It is the direct answer to look-through: shocks do not roll off
    cleanly if new ones keep arriving. Jul 31 made the demand-side argument
    instead. Aug 27 adds the persistence-of-supply argument, so both exits
    from look-through are now in her record.

Own language — expectations and "despair":
  "the real problem with us missing on our inflation objective for so long is
   the risk that an inflationary mindset starts to set in with the public."
  → "Inflationary mindset" is carry-over from Jun 2 ("Preventing an
    inflationary mindset is critical").
  "If you look at inflation expectations, whether it's market based measures
   or survey based measures, it does seem like individuals are still focused
   that we will deliver on that 2%."
  → She concedes measured expectations are anchored. This is the only
    reassuring sentence in the interview.
  Erie, PA worker roundtable: "they were all saying that they're feeling a
   sense of despair ... One fellow said that he'd always gone to all of his
   son's football games, and this spring he couldn't do the travel football
   because the price of gasoline was just so high."
  "maybe we're starting to get some of that inflationary mindset set in. I
   don't think we're there yet, but that's what I want to make sure we avoid."
  → "Despair" is carried over from Jul 31 ("consumers are expressing despair")
    and now has a concrete source. The anecdote is about energy prices, a
    supply shock. She uses it as evidence of mindset risk, not as something to
    look through.
  → "I don't think we're there yet" bounds the claim. Her case is
    preventive, not that expectations have already de-anchored.

Shift vs. prior speech (Jul 31):  HAWKISH LEAN — adds persistence of layered
  supply shocks to the Jul 31 demand-side argument, and reads an in-line PCE
  print as about 3 percent inflation. Measured expectations conceded as
  anchored.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Jun 2):       HAWKISH — Jun 2 framed the hike case as
  conditional on trends; Aug 27 says the persistence is already visible.

MONETARY POLICY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
FOMC statement language echoed:
  "The Committee will deliver price stability." [Jul 29 stmt]
  → Hammack: "am I hearing them still believe that we have our 2% objective
    and that we're going to deliver on that 2% objective?"

Own language — KEY (the call):
  "we've got a lot of time before our next meeting. So I don't want to
   prejudge anything, but I believe now is the time to act."
  → Jul 31: "Now is the time for the FOMC to act." Verbatim carry-over from
    her dissent statement, restated three weeks later after one more inflation
    print. The pre-meeting hedge ("I don't want to prejudge") is formal. The
    position is unchanged.

Own language — the stance:
  "I don't see any restriction in policy."
  "I think it's appropriate for us to put some restraint there to help bring
   inflation back down to target."
  → Escalation in the restrictiveness verdict:
      Jun 2:  "monetary policy may not be sufficiently restrictive"
      Jul 31: "I did not see the current policy stance as appropriately
              restrictive"
      Aug 27: "I don't see ANY restriction in policy"
    "Not appropriately restrictive" becomes "no restriction at all." That
    places the July policy rate at or below neutral in her judgment.
  → "Put SOME restraint there" implies moving from zero restraint into
    restrictive territory. That is more than one 25bp step from a neutral
    starting point. [Inference: she does not name a size or path. The words
    point beyond a single move, but she does not say so.]

Own language — neutral:
  "I think neutral is one of those things that you know it when you see it.
   It's more an experiential thing ... My estimate tends to be towards the
   higher side of of people on the committee in terms of where I think it is."
  → First time Hammack has placed her neutral estimate relative to the
    committee. A high neutral estimate is what makes "no restriction" coherent
    at 3.50–3.75 percent. She names no number, and the clip cuts off before
    she finishes.
  → Pairs with Goolsbee's later "Sasquatch" framing of r-star (Sept 21) and the
    Chair's Sept 16 refusal to use neutral operationally. Hammack reaches the
    same place by a different route: judge restrictiveness by market feel,
    not by model estimates.

Cost of delay:
  "The longer inflation stays above our objective, the harder it will be for
   us to bring it back down. And the more pain that individuals and
   businesses are going to be experiencing."
  → Carry-over from Jul 31 ("more challenging and costly") with "pain" added.

  Hike signal: PRESENT — "now is the time to act," unconditional apart from
               the formal "I don't want to prejudge."
  Cut signal:  ABSENT.

Scenario framing: DESCRIPTIVE — the "neatly roll off ... or new shocks"
  question describes a risk without naming a policy response for each branch.

Shift vs. prior speech (Jul 31):  HAWKISH — the same call to act, with the
  restrictiveness verdict hardened from "not appropriately restrictive" to
  "no restriction," and a high neutral estimate disclosed.
Shift vs. pre-FOMC (Jun 2):       HAWKISH — conditional "may soon be
  appropriate" has become "now is the time."

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SUMMARY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Net signal:    HAWKISH — confirmation of the July dissent, with the stance
               verdict hardened.

               Three weeks after dissenting for a hike, Hammack repeats her
               July 31 line word for word: "now is the time to act." She
               reads the July PCE print as about 3 percent inflation. The new
               material is the stance. "Not appropriately restrictive" on July
               31 becomes "I don't see any restriction in policy." She
               discloses that her neutral estimate is on the high side of the
               committee, which is what makes that verdict coherent. She adds
               a persistence argument, "layered shocks" that may not "neatly
               roll off," to her July demand-side case.

               The one reassuring note is that measured inflation expectations
               still look anchored ("I don't think we're there yet"). Her case
               is to prevent an inflationary mindset, not to reverse one.

Position relative to committee:
  Hammack's call was confirmed twenty days later, when the Committee hiked
  12–0. Her "no restriction" verdict matches the Chair's Sept 16 position
  that the hike "removed a dose of accommodation." That puts the pre-hike
  rate below neutral, as she had argued. Her "layered shocks" framing sits
  alongside Kashkari's successive shocks and later appears in her own Sept 24
  remarks ("the shocks arrive one after another").

Language watch:
  • "I don't see any restriction in policy" — stance at or below neutral.
    Stage: COMMITTEE-WIDE by Sept 16 (Chair: "removed a dose of
    accommodation").
  • "layered shocks" — persistence of successive supply shocks. Stage:
    EMERGING (Kashkari Jul 31; Hammack Aug 27 and Sept 24; Barkin; Goolsbee
    Sept 21).
  • "towards the higher side ... on the committee" (neutral) — Hammack's
    first relative placement. Stage: DISPERSED.
  • "now is the time to act" — carry-over from Jul 31. Resolved by the Sept 16
    hike.

Analyst flag:  YES
  → (1) "Put some restraint there" starts from "no restriction." Does she
    think one 25bp move got policy into restrictive territory, or only to
    neutral? Her Sept 25 panel is the first chance to ask.
  → (2) The clip cuts off mid-answer on neutral. CNBC may have aired a longer
    version. The full answer could contain a number.
