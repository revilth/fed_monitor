Speech
The Economic Mixdown
November 23, 2026
John C. Williams
, President and Chief Executive Officer
Remarks at the University of Mount Saint Vincent, Bronx, New York
As prepared for delivery
Introduction
Thank you for the kind  introduction. I’ll add that while I have many responsibilities as president of  the New York Fed, there are two that are always a highlight for me. The first  is when I travel around the Federal Reserve’s Second District to meet with  community, business, and civic leaders. And the second is when I speak with  groups of students who are interested in learning more about the economy and  economics.
And today, I get to do  both. It’s been a wonderful day here in the Bronx. Thank you to the faculty and  students at Mount Saint Vincent for putting this event together. This is truly  a beautiful campus.
Today, I’ll share some  economic data and discuss how the Federal Reserve is working to achieve its dual  mandate goals. I’ll also explain how AI is showing up in multiple areas of the economy  and offer my economic outlook.
Now is when I give the  official disclaimer that the views you will hear today are mine alone and do  not necessarily reflect those of the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) or  others in the Federal Reserve System.
Two Tracks of the U.S. Economy
I’ll mention another  thing that I love to do in this job—and that is to weave music lyrics into the  text of the speeches that I give. I’ve been known to quote Earth, Wind &  Fire, Led Zeppelin, Rihanna, Taylor Swift, and the Beatles. I know, that’s  quite the repertoire.
But today, let’s do  something a bit different. Instead of playing DJ by choosing a specific song, let’s  think of ourselves as audio mixing engineers—because analyzing the economy is similar.  Like an engineer mixing multiple tracks or audio elements into a single stereo  file, a policymaker pulls together economic data, surveys, and other indicators  to make up the entirety of an outlook.
Every great music mix  starts with a good beat. So, here’s where I begin.
Right now, the data continue  to show a resilient economy. Despite multiple large shocks and elevated  uncertainty, the underlying momentum in the U.S. economy is strong.
In fact, overall growth appears  to be trending upward. Last year, real GDP increased by about 2 percent. And so  far, this year, the economy has been growing at about a 2-3/4 percent annual  rate. Productivity growth is running well above pre-pandemic levels. And  business investment has surged, powered by the ever-expanding AI buildout.  Expectations of solid growth and high productivity have, in turn, fueled stock  market gains, leading to robust consumer spending, particularly for  higher-income households and homeowners.
Although much of the overall  strength in the economy can be traced back to the AI-related boom and consumer  spending, there is more to it. To borrow another audio mixing term, we’re  seeing a “two-track” economy. On one side, important parts of the economy are  very strong. But on the other, the economy is growing at or below its trend  rate.
Sectors like residential  investment and non-AI related structures investment have weakened further over  the past several quarters. This weakness also showed up in recent surveys we  conducted with businesses around the Second District, who report that while  manufacturing is strong, business activity in the services sector fell sharply.
The Labor Market Channel
I’ll now layer on  another track. You likely have heard about the Fed’s dual mandate. This means  that our work is guided by two goals: maximum employment and price stability.  Think of this as a two-channel music mixer that blends two input sources into a  single output.
When it comes to  employment, the data show that the labor market continues to be remarkably  solid, with no signs of significant reacceleration, or deceleration for that  matter.
Several key indicators demonstrate  this resilience. The unemployment rate has fluctuated in a narrow band in the  low four percent range for the past several months. Layoff rates are near  historic lows. Payroll employment gains have been positive. With job separations  and hiring remaining muted, we continue to be in a low-fire, low-hire  environment, with labor market conditions broadly in balance.
On a more local level,  the New York Fed’s regional business surveys indicate that employment in the  Second District is picking up at a solid clip in the manufacturing sector but  only holding steady in the services sector.
1
In these surveys, we also  ask regional businesses about their AI adoption and its effects on their  workforces. So far, they report that they are retraining employees to use AI  rather than eliminating jobs.
2
And in New York City in  particular, where the employment profile was once dominated by healthcare and  finance, we see a growing technology sector that is employing an increasing  number of workers. The strength in the tech sector has supported the robust rebound  of commercial real estate.
The Inflation Channel
On balance, there’s  resilience in economic growth and demand, and that’s also translated into a  stable labor market.
But inflation is simply too high.
Inflation is currently  about 3-1/2 percent, well above the FOMC’s definition for price stability of 2  percent.
3
This high inflation is  because over the past year and a half, a very unusual set of shocks has  affected the economy, driven primarily by three factors. The first is higher  tariffs on imported goods. The second is supply-chain disruptions and higher  energy and commodity prices due to the conflicts in the Middle East and  elsewhere. And the third is robust demand for certain categories of goods and  services associated with the surge in AI-related investments.
The encouraging news is  that the effects of tariffs on inflation have largely abated and should not add  significantly to inflation in goods prices in the future. Of course, this could  change if new tariffs were introduced.
Energy prices remain elevated,  and the outlook is highly uncertain. The ongoing conflict in the Middle East  and severe capacity constraints in oil refining have driven up not only crude  oil prices, but also the spreads of prices for refined products like gasoline  and diesel fuel relative to oil prices.
And of course, there’s  AI. The AI boom has resulted in a race between available supply and surging  demand in certain categories of goods—and so far, demand is clearly outpacing  supply. Prices of goods essential for the AI buildout have increased sharply,  especially for products like semiconductors and chips that are needed to  support the rapid expansion.
Fortunately, other  indicators are more encouraging regarding the inflation outlook. Prices for  housing services have decelerated, and there is no evidence that the labor  market, and wage growth in particular, are adding to inflationary pressures.  This conclusion is supported by a wide range of data on labor compensation, as  well as the New York Fed’s HPW Labor Market Tightness Index.
4
Importantly, survey- and  market-based measures show that medium- and longer-term inflation expectations  remain well anchored.
5
Although the effects of  tariffs, geopolitical conflicts, and the AI surge are showing up in prices of  certain categories of goods, there is little evidence of these spilling over significantly  into broader and more persistent inflation.
Frequency EQ: Monetary Policy and the  Economic Outlook
This leaves us at an unusual  macroeconomic juncture. The economic data are strong. The labor market is  stable. And this strength is not creating macroeconomic imbalances in the labor  market. Despite this overall balance, high inflation is proving persistent. So,  the risk to maximum employment is dialed down, but the risk to achieving price  stability is dialed up.
AI is a big driver of  economic strength, but the magnitude and timing of its effects are still highly  uncertain. On the one hand, the pace of the AI buildout could slow, which could  reverse much of the AI-driven inflation. On the other hand, investment demand  could accelerate further, leading to stronger growth and putting further upward  pressure on inflation, especially if supply can’t keep up.
While AI risks are  two-sided and roughly balanced, the supply-side risks to inflation, such as  those related to the conflict in the Middle East and supply chain bottlenecks,  are more clearly skewed to the upside.
What does this mean for  monetary policy?
Monetary policy cannot  move ships or reopen pipelines and refineries, but it can—and must—ensure that  these shocks do not lead to broader or persistent inflationary dynamics or  erode well-anchored inflation expectations. Importantly, monetary policy works  with a lag of one to two years for its maximal effect on the economy. Therefore,  it is essential that we remain forward-looking and not overly focused on inflation’s  path in the past.
It is imperative that we  restore inflation to our 2 percent target in a timely manner and on a sustained  basis. The statement released following the most recent FOMC meeting said: “The  accumulation of more information will help the Committee determine the policy  stance appropriate to ensure a timely return of inflation to the Committee’s 2  percent goal.”
6
The data that we have received since then show that the economy has remained  strong and that inflation has continued to run too high.
In terms of my outlook,  I expect real GDP growth to increase about 2-3/4 percent this year and to be near  that pace next year, reflecting resilient demand and strong productivity  growth. With this ongoing momentum in the economy, I expect the unemployment  rate to edge down to about 4 percent over the next year.
Reflecting the effects  of elevated energy and AI-related goods prices, I expect overall inflation to  come in at about 3-1/2 percent this year. As the effects of tariffs move  further into the rearview mirror, energy prices normalize, and the demand and  supply of AI-related goods move back toward better balance, I anticipate  inflation will slow to just above 2 percent next year, then reach our  longer-run inflation goal of 2 percent in 2028.
Conclusion
An audio producer would tell  me that now is the time for the “mixdown”—when I combine and balance all that  I’ve said so far.
The economy is strong  and resilient, despite large shocks and elevated uncertainty. But getting  inflation back to 2 percent is 100 percent essential. We must take actions over  time that will ensure that we get inflation back to our 2 percent goal in a  timely manner and on a sustained basis. There is still work to do to get that  done. As I consider the future path of monetary policy, I will continue to study  the totality of the data to assess the underlying trends in inflation and the  balance of supply and demand in the economy.
This has been a great  mixing session. I look forward to your questions.
