  Good afternoon. I’m delighted to join you for the 25th annual Boston Fed conference for 

regional & community banks. Twenty-five years is a great milestone, and the constructive work of 

the Fed and regional and community banks extends back much further than that – and will 

continue, going forward.  

  Regional and community banks are key economic actors on the “Main Streets” of New 

England. With the Fed, you share a commitment to a safe and sound banking system, an ever-

improving payments and financial infrastructure, and the region’s economic vibrancy.  

  Those are fairly timeless notions – and may have been discussed at the first annual 

conference. The agenda for today’s 25th conference underlines the ongoing evolution that the 

region’s banks, and the central bank, are attuned to – with topics like fraud risk management in the 

age of A.I., new data resources illuminating banking trends, and forward-looking discussions around 

payments and emerging financial technologies. Important, complex issues such as these require 

constructive engagement and collaboration.  

In our time today, I’ll begin with some comments before sitting down for a discussion with 

Steven Fay. My remarks will include my views on the national economy and monetary policy, key 

aspects of banking trends and conditions in our region, and some brief perspective on the Boston 

Fed’s regional engagement – an important dimension of our nation’s central bank scope of 

responsibilities.  

  As I begin, let me note as always that these are my own views. I’m not speaking for any of 

my colleagues at other Federal Reserve Banks or the Board of Governors. 

Perspectives On the National Economy and Monetary Policy 

I’ll turn now to the national economy, outlining my views on economic conditions before 

discussing my outlook, and implications for monetary policy. The big picture is that the U.S. 

economy has shown remarkable resilience despite significant adverse shocks from tariffs and 

energy. 

  Starting with the labor market, conditions are generally stable and broadly consistent with 

achieving the employment side of the Fed’s Congressional mandate – that is, conditions are near 

maximum, or full, employment. In particular, the unemployment rate remains low, having fluctuated 

in a narrow range just above 4 percent for more than two years, a pattern consistent with overall 

balance between labor demand and supply.

In addition, hiring has accelerated somewhat this year, relative to its sluggish pace in the 

second half of 2025. It is possible this pickup reflects some momentum that could prove sustained. 

For instance, the average work week, which can be a leading indicator of employment growth, has 

risen some. However, recent job gains could simply reflect headcounts normalizing in several 

sectors, after a period of weak hiring due to elevated economic uncertainty. Here, I’ll note that we 

have not yet seen a sustained, broad-based increase in job openings that could signal an 

overheating labor market. Moreover, wage pressures remain subdued, and tighter conditions may 

only be emerging in a few sectors heavily exposed to the AI buildout, such as construction and 

durable goods manufacturing.  

  Taking these, and other, signals into account, I see the U.S. labor market as broadly 

balanced – though the aggregate data mask significant variation across place and sector. Most 

workers looking for a job are able to find one, although it may take time. The data do not suggest 

that job seekers are increasingly becoming discouraged, and the labor force participation rate is 

holding up well, despite an aging labor force.  

  Turning to the risks to the labor market, I see them as two-sided. On the upside, I have 

already mentioned the potential for a more sustained acceleration in hiring, which could lead to 

growth in labor demand outpacing growth in labor supply. And on the downside, amid much 

uncertainty, widespread AI adoption could limit firms’ demand for workers.   

  But for now, a stable labor market near full employment is helping to sustain relatively robust 

consumer spending, with gains in household wealth from equity and housing valuations also 

providing important support. In addition, we are seeing considerable strength in firms’ capital 

outlays, mostly related to the AI buildout. Given resilient consumer demand and strong business 

investment, GDP growth was somewhat stronger in the first half of this year than many forecasters 

had expected – and seems to have stepped-up further in the third quarter. 

  The recent GDP data could signal somewhat more buoyant underlying growth than the 

relative balance suggested by the labor market data. A potential concern is that the acceleration in 

GDP might reflect an increase in demand that could strain supply and put upward pressure on 

prices. However, this is not my current expectation. Rather, I see the strength in real economic 

activity as reflecting not just strong demand but also an increase in the economy’s productive 

capacity due to efficiency gains. That is, an economy that can now grow faster with the same 

number of workers. Indeed, output per hour – or labor productivity – accelerated notably after the 

pandemic; and recent developments, including early efficiencies from AI adoption, remain 

consistent with an expansion in the economy’s overall productive capacity. As a result, even with

stable labor market conditions, there can be faster GDP growth with demand and supply remaining 

roughly balanced. In this context, the implications for inflation would be more benign. 

  Turning to prices, inflation is notably above the Fed’s 2 percent inflation target and has been 

for five and a half years. But while recent inflation readings include some promising news, other 

developments have been less favorable.    

  Over the 12 months ending in August, total PCE inflation – the Fed’s preferred measure – 

was 3.4 percent. Core PCE inflation, which omits the important but volatile food and energy 

categories but can provide a better gauge of where inflation is headed, was a bit lower at 3 percent. 

Focusing on the more recent data, annualized three-month core PCE inflation was only about 2 

percent in August. The improvement in short-term inflation measures partly reflects the waning 

effects from tariffs and from last spring’s energy shock. 

  However, I expect both total and core inflation to again run above the Fed’s 2 percent target 

in the coming months, given the recent jump in prices of refined energy products like jet and diesel 

fuel, and home heating oil. These price increases should keep inflation elevated in the 

transportation sector and in other sectors such as lodging and related services, where energy is an 

important input. We are also likely to see continued price pressures for items related to building out 

data centers.   

  Against this backdrop, it will be important to monitor whether elevated inflation remains 

largely confined to energy and AI-buildout-related categories, while price growth continues to 

moderate elsewhere – or whether evidence of price pressures broadens. 

  Taken together, what are the implications of all these developments for my outlook? My 

modal scenario – the one I see as most likely – continues to feature healthy GDP growth, with the 

labor market remaining near full employment. In this scenario, inflation gradually returns to 2 

percent, as continued efficiency gains support the balance between demand and supply, and 

contribute to disinflation. 

  But several other scenarios are also plausible – and I see two as particularly salient. Each 

seems almost as likely as my modal outlook, and each highlights the risk of inflation staying above 

target for longer.  

In one scenario, stronger-than-expected demand from AI generates even greater investment 

in data centers. The related prospect of higher productivity fosters continued stock price increases, 

further boosting household spending. As a result, even with additional efficiency gains, demand 

outpaces supply, the labor market overheats, and inflation remains persistently above target.

  The other scenario includes additional pressures from tariffs, energy prices – or both – and 

inflation challenges that become even more acute. In particular, the lack of a resolution to the 

conflict in the Middle East reinforces this possibility. It is also important to note that continued 

elevated energy prices could boost domestic oil production. As a result, unlike energy shock 

episodes prior to the 2000s, higher inflation might not be accompanied by a significant decline in 

economic activity.  

  With elevated risks of persistently high inflation, but more limited downside risks to the real 

economy and labor market, I believe it is appropriate for the Federal Open Market Committee (the 

FOMC) to focus on the inflation side of the Fed’s dual mandate. Therefore, I supported last month’s 

FOMC decision to raise the federal funds rate by 25 basis points. I also currently expect that it will 

be appropriate to raise rates a bit further before the end of the year to ensure a timely and durable 

return of inflation to the Committee’s 2 percent target. However, my view of the need for further 

policy action, and the appropriate timing, will depend importantly on how the totality of incoming 

data shapes my outlook and the associated balance of risks.  

Banking Trends and Conditions in New England 

I will turn now to some of the trends we see in First District community banking conditions. 

Banks in New England continue to strengthen their communities and successfully navigate a highly 

competitive market, both for loans and deposits. While loan growth remains subdued for community 

banks in the region, loan performance continues to be strong, with non-accruals to total loans below 

long-term averages. In a welcome sign and despite continued funding pressures, earnings at 

community banks in New England have also improved over the past year from long-term lows. 

  New England community banks have a distinct asset composition relative to their national 

counterparts, in large part due to the prevalence of mutual banks in the region. Community banks in 

the First District have a higher percentage of assets in the form of loans, in particular 1-4 family 

residential loans, as compared to their national peers. While this has the benefit of increased 

residential investment in the communities they serve, it also introduces trade-offs that need to be 

well managed, in particular trade-offs related to earnings and liquidity. 

  We continue to closely monitor how banks in the region manage their interest rate risk 

profiles, especially given the recent increase in the federal funds rate and a higher median funds 

rate path implied by the Summary of Economic Projections released in September. Over the past 

two years, funding costs for interest-bearing accounts at community banks have largely stabilized –

both in the region and across the nation, albeit at higher rates than over the preceding decade. In 

another positive development, local core deposit ratios have modestly improved as well.  

  On the lending side, yields have continued to increase from pandemic-period lows through 

new loans at higher rates and a high concentration of loans with 5-year rate terms that have begun 

to reprice. This has improved regional banks’ profitability, as their net interest margins returned to 

levels closer to post-global-financial crisis norms. However, New England remains a highly 

competitive lending environment, and overall returns for community banks in the region remain 

below those of national peers, continuing a long-term pattern.   

  Competition and the need to spread the cost of operations have been cited as motivating 

factors in recent M&A activity in the region. However, mergers in the district have slowed from the 

recent high seen last year, at least partially driven by an improved earnings environment. At five 

mergers to this point in the year, the M&A trend looks more similar to the 2021-24 period than 2025, 

when we had fifteen community bank mergers in our region.   

  With respect to supervisory policy, over the past year, Federal Reserve Board of Governors’ 

Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle Bowman has initiated impactful changes to modernize the 

regulatory and supervisory landscape.1 Locally, we are applying the Board’s Statement of 

Supervisory Operating Principles,2 consistent with the delegated nature of banking supervision 

responsibilities at the Reserve Banks. We also continue to maintain strong partnerships with our 

state counterparts and want to thank the many leaders from New England state banking authorities 

who attended the conference today. 

  A final observation about regional and community banks. Often as I travel the region and 

hear from a wide range of stakeholders, they express appreciation for the engagement of banks in 

the economic “fabric” of their communities. I heard this recently from people involved in economic 

development efforts in New Hampshire, who mentioned partnering with community banks to reach 

small to “micro” business borrowers. And in Connecticut, I heard recently from entrepreneurs 

developing childcare facilities about the importance of relationships with trusted banks, who can 

provide business insights as well as credit.

Our Engagement as a Regional Reserve Bank  

  Today I’ve discussed the economy and the banking environment; I’ll conclude with a brief 

observation on another thing worth valuing and leveraging – the unique structure of the U.S. central 

bank and the regional identity and engagement of the Reserve Banks.3  

  We are fortunate in the U.S. to have a central bank with regional reserve banks that play a 

number of roles, including hearing from stakeholders to understand economic and financial 

conditions, rounding out the extensive data analysis we do. Regional “rootedness” and engagement 

not only help us understand current conditions, but also people’s expectations about the future – 

especially around our mandated goals, stable prices and maximum employment. I and other 

regional Reserve Bank presidents bring these insights to the monetary policymaking table in 

Washington. 

  Research and analysis – with a rigorously empirical and nonpartisan mindset – allows the 

Reserve Banks to provide valuable insights on issues, challenges, and opportunities affecting the 

regional economy and labor markets – helping to further our vision of a vibrant economy that works 

for everyone. While we avoid veering into solutioning that should be left to elected fiscal 

policymakers, we contribute to economic vibrancy by serving as a researcher and resource. I will 

highlight three of many recent examples.   

  This summer, we released a comprehensive survey of household wealth in Massachusetts 

(“MassECHOS”), which can help the public, private, philanthropic, and nonprofit sector 

understand opportunities to consider – and helps the Fed see more clearly the prevailing 

conditions of the households that our monetary policymaking impacts.4  

  We also shared research on the availability and cost of childcare in New England. This 

impacts the ability of many to participate in the labor market – and thus relates directly to our 

achievement of the mandate goal Congress assigned the Fed, maximum employment.5

Closing Observations 

In closing, a vibrant economy that works for all – our vision at the Boston Fed – requires a 

safe, sound, resilient financial and banking system. One that is engaged with, and responsive to, its 

constituents and region. 

In that spirit, I appreciate the work that New England bankers do – and your commitment to 

our region’s economy and providing services and credit to New Englanders. And I value the 

constructive way you engage with the Fed in our roles as regulator, supervisor, monetary 

policymaker, researcher, convener, and provider of financial infrastructure and payments services. 

  Thank you for being here. My best for the year ahead.
