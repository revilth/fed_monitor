Key Takeaways 

1. The Boston Fed’s 70th economic conference focuses on the U.S. housing market. The 
conference brings together scholars, policymakers, and practitioners to consider this important 
part of the U.S. economy. The Fed does not set housing policy, but its research helps people 
understand how housing markets work. 

“Building a shared understanding of these [housing market] issues [are] essential – and a fundamental 
reason for conferences like this,” Collins says. “Rigorous research can help inform better decisions and 
identify potential solutions.” 

2. Housing matters for the Federal Reserve’s mission, vision, and mandate from Congress. Housing 
is an important channel through which monetary policy affects the economy, can contribute 
disproportionately to business cycles, and can present a barrier to economic vibrancy. 

“Housing is a central component of a vibrant economy that works for all,” Collins says. 
“There is no ‘silver bullet’ that will solve the many challenges in the housing market,” she says. “It will take 
constructive collaboration among many parties.” 

3. Higher interest rates can slow housing construction and make homes more expensive to buy. 
But tighter monetary policy is sometimes necessary to achieve stable prices, which benefit 
housing, and the economy, as a whole, in the long run.  

“Over the longer run, stable prices create predictable economic conditions that are good for housing,” 
Collins says. 

4. High housing costs are hurting households and employers. Adequate and affordable housing 
has become a central challenge for many families and firms in New England and across the nation. 
High costs can limit homeownership and families’ associated wealth-building potential.   

“A lack of available and affordable housing [for workers] can prevent local businesses from expanding,” 
Collins says.    

5. Understanding both housing supply and demand is key. Once largely a local concern, housing 
supply is now a national issue, and supply conditions have become increasingly important in 
policy discussions. Both construction productivity and zoning can contribute to housing supply 
shortages, and adding to the housing stock can take many years. 

“I’ll note that monetary policy also plays a role here,” Collins says. “[While essential at times to ensure price 
stability,] tighter policy in the near term can limit the expansion of the housing stock needed to ease 
housing market pressures. It can also affect consumers’ willingness and ability to obtain a mortgage and, 
hence, housing demand.” 

6. Measuring both quantities and costs in the housing sector is challenging. Estimates of the 
housing shortage vary widely, due largely to differing assumptions about household formation 
trends and normal vacancy rates. Economists also debate the best way to measure the true costs 
of housing for both owners and renters.  

“The shortage is not just about one number. It looks quite different across places, both here in New 
England and around the country,” Collins says. “The shortage also varies along demographic dimensions, 
including by income and age.”

Good morning. It is a pleasure to welcome all of you to the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston and to 

welcome those viewing the conference livestream. 

As we begin, I want to thank Egon Zakrajšek, our director of research, and his colleagues for 

organizing a timely and substantive conference – especially the three economists serving as 

conference sponsors: Daniel Cooper, Chris Foote, and Paul Willen. My thanks as well to our 

organizing team in the Research department and to the many others at the Bank who support this 

gathering. 

For the 70th time, the Boston Fed is proud to host an economic conference bringing together 

scholars, practitioners, and policymakers to study an important topic. Rigorous empirical research is 

a hallmark of our conferences, and we’ll be discussing a great deal of data. However, I would like to 

begin with something more anecdotal. I frequently travel around our district and make a point of 

meeting with a wide range of people in each New England state every year. Housing is almost 

always mentioned in these discussions – its cost and availability; its impact on household budgets; 

and, crucially for businesses, how a lack of affordable housing affects their ability to attract and 

retain workers.   

Over the next day and a half, this conference will explore key challenges facing the U.S. housing 

market through multiple lenses. Along with scholarly analysis, practitioners will share their on-the-

ground insights about housing-related issues in New England and across the nation. 

Why have a Boston Fed conference focused on housing? 

To set the stage for the conference, I’d like to provide a few observations. But first, my standard 

disclaimer: these are my own views, and may not be those of my colleagues at the Board of 

Governors or the other Reserve Banks. In particular, I’ll briefly and broadly address the Federal 

Reserve’s connections to housing and reflect on why the Boston Fed is holding a conference 

focused on housing – its challenges and potential solutions.  

In this spirit, I’ll make four points. 

First, housing is a central component of a vibrant economy that works for all – that is the vision we 

pursue at the Boston Fed. As I noted at last year’s State of the Nation’s Housing event, rapid price 

and rent increases over the past five years have created enormous pressure on household

Moreover, a lack of available and affordable housing can prevent local businesses from expanding.

housing. Increasingly, I also hear about housing projects slowing or stalling due to a lack of skilled

Of course, monetary policy decisions also matter for housing. In the near term, the higher interest 

rates sometimes needed to cool the economy and keep inflation in check can make homes more 

expensive to buy and to build. But over the longer run, stable prices create predictable economic 

conditions that are good for housing. 

A second observation: housing can contribute disproportionately to business cycles and is an 

important transmission channel for monetary policy. Although housing accounts for a relatively 

small share of GDP, it drove about 17 percent of GDP growth during the mid-2000s housing boom 

— and about 41 percent of the output contraction during the subsequent bust.2 And since the 

construction of new homes and buyers’ willingness and ability to purchase them are particularly 

sensitive to interest rates, monetary policy can have an outsized influence on the housing sector. 

When the Fed began tightening monetary policy in 2022, mortgage rates rose sharply – and higher 

rates have slowed new residential investment, a point I will return to shortly.

Themes to Explore at this Conference 

Turning to the conference itself, I would like to highlight two of its overarching themes. The first is 

the complexities of housing supply. Researchers – including several in this room – have studied 

housing supply for decades, and communities have long debated it at the local level. But recently, 

housing supply has become a central focus of the national housing policy conversation, alongside 

housing demand. The second theme I’ll cover is the difficulty of measuring important housing 

variables. 

Theme 1 — Supply

demand. While trends in supply can be difficult to discern at times, especially those related to 

productivity, supply conditions play an important role in understanding the dynamics of the overall 

economy. Notably, over the past several years, supply factors have been important drivers of 

aggregate economic dynamics. Pandemic-related supply chain constraints, energy shocks tied to

Theme 2 — Measurement 

Measurement is another important issue related to housing – and a topic I care deeply about.  

I spoke about measurement last year at the National Association for Business Economists’ (NABE) 

economic measurement seminar.16 In the housing sector, measuring both prices and quantities is 

difficult. However, accurate measurement is important for understanding housing market dynamics 

– both nationally and regionally – as well as the sectoral challenges we face.  

Starting with prices, shelter is the largest component of the Consumer Price Index,17 and shelter 

inflation was a key reason inflation stayed elevated following the pandemic. In 2024, an important 

question was whether elevated housing inflation reflected new price pressures or simply existing 

leases catching up to market rents – something our standard housing market metrics made hard to 

discern in real time.18  

There is also a deeper, conceptual question when it comes to measuring shelter costs. While most 

Americans own their homes, our housing-related inflation measures capture housing costs largely

With respect to quantities, I’ll highlight that estimates of the national housing shortage range from 

several million units to none at all.19 However, the shortage is not just about one number. It looks 

quite different across places, both here in New England and around the country. Shortages also 

vary along demographic dimensions, including by income and age. And not every housing unit is 

the same. New England has some of the oldest housing in the country, and an aging, deteriorating 

housing stock can provide less housing than a simple count of units would suggest.20 Elena Patel’s 

session on measuring the housing shortage, which follows my remarks, is an excellent place to 

start. 

Closing Observations 

In conclusion, I’ll note that there is no “silver bullet” that will solve the many challenges facing 

housing today. It will take constructive collaboration among many parties. Building a shared 

understanding of the complexities of the housing market is essential – and a fundamental reason 

for conferences like this. Rigorous research can help inform better decisions and identify potential 

solutions.   

As I said in my Joint Center remarks, “housing is deeply tied to the overarching role of the Federal 

Reserve – supporting a strong, stable, and healthy economy and financial system, for the benefit of 

the public we at the Fed are here to serve.”21 I am grateful for the opportunity for all of us to learn 

more about this important topic over the coming day and a half. We have a great lineup of 

conference speakers, panelists, and discussants, and I look forward to the presentations and 

discussions. 

It is now my pleasure to welcome the participants of our first session to the stage – Elena Patel, the 

paper author; Paul Willen, the discussant; and Daniel Cooper, our moderator.   

Thank you.
