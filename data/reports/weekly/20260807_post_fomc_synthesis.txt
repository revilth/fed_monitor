THE HEADLINE — WHAT THIS CYCLE ACTUALLY SHOWED
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The 9–3 vote is a poor description of where the committee is.

Three things emerged in nine days that the vote count conceals:

 1. THE MAJORITY IS SPLIT ON THE STATEMENT'S OWN INFLATION SENTENCE. Paulson
    (median voter) defends the supply-shock attribution and the look-through it
    implies. Cook, voting the same way one day later, declines to defend it:
    energy "contributed significantly … but it is not the only factor." The
    fault line runs INSIDE the nine, not between the nine and the three.

 2. THE MOST COMMITTED-SOUNDING DISSENTER DESCRIBES HIS OWN VOTE AS MARGINAL.
    Kashkari, whose July 31 written statement reads as settled doctrine, said on
    August 5 that he went into the meeting UNDECIDED and explicitly denied that
    waiting until September would have been too late. September pricing built
    off the dissent count is probably overstating the bloc's firmness — for him.
    Hammack and Logan have made no equivalent admission; do not read it across.

 3. THE HAWKISH TAIL IS FATTER THAN THE VOTE SUGGESTS. Daly — who endorsed the
    hold "completely" — pre-commits to a response LARGER than any dissenter has
    requested, explicitly ruling out "small … almost surgical calibrations" as
    insufficient, conditional on evidence of compounding. The committee's
    reaction function is more aggressive than its vote.

Net committee lean: HAWKISH, and hardening — but through the option set rather
than through the verdict. Every official who spoke carries a tightening
conditional and NONE mentions cuts in any form. Cuts have disappeared from the
committee's spoken vocabulary entirely. That is the cleanest single fact of the
cycle.

THE CENTRAL ANALYTICAL DISPUTE: IS PRICING POWER A LEVEL OR A COUNTDOWN?
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
This is the most decision-relevant disagreement to emerge, and it is not
between hawks and doves — it is between two officials who voted the same way.

  PAULSON (Aug 4), as evidence policy IS restrictive:
    "I hear from price setters, CEOs of big companies, especially ones that are
     selling directly to consumers, that they don't feel like they can pass
     along cost increases to consumers because consumers aren't in a position to
     be able to absorb it. … That's another thing that makes me feel like we're
     in the mildly restrictive space."

  DALY (Aug 6), same fact, opposite horizon:
    Cites the identical evidence — NY Fed services and Richmond Fed
    manufacturing surveys showing expected costs above expected selling
    prices — as reason #1 for the benign scenario. Then, as reason #2 for the
    adverse scenario:
    "producer prices are just outpacing consumer prices … PPI in June is 5.5 and
     consumer price index grows by 3.5 … the cumulative change still leaves
     firms behind … at some point firms want to maintain their margins. They
     don't want to take it out of their profits. … they would love to be able to
     pass these things along."
    Plus a contagion mechanism: "if the firm across the street from me is heavily
     energy dependent and raising prices and the firm down the street is heavily
     affected by tariffs and raising prices, I might just get into the ethos of
     everybody else is raising prices. Why don't I try to? That hasn't happened
     yet to our ability to discern it. But I think it's not a risk we can just
     push aside."

  → Pricing power is a STOCK that depletes. Paulson reads the level; Daly reads
    the countdown. Both are correct about the data. The question is whether
    compressed margins are evidence of restriction working or of pass-through
    building.
  → This is the strongest analytical objection to the majority's position, and
    it comes from someone who endorsed the hold. It is also directly testable
    against Barkin's Richmond manufacturer contacts — which is a further reason
    the Barkin gap matters.

TWO QUANTIFIED TRIP WIRES — NEW THIS CYCLE, AND THEY POINT OPPOSITE WAYS
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
For the first time this cycle, officials put NUMBERS on their decision
variables instead of adjectives. Both are trackable; they measure different
things and imply different September decisions.

  PAULSON — underlying inflation, 2.4–2.8% (the benign trip wire):
    "I'm trying to measure the depth of water in a pool and there's rocks being
     thrown into the pool — tariffs, energy shocks. So what's the level of the
     pool? When I look at underlying inflation, when you try to strip out the
     effects of those things, I see it somewhere in the range of 2.4, 2.8."
    → Against a 3.7% headline, the median voter's working problem is 0.4–0.8pp
      above target, not 1.7pp. This is why her vote "was not a close call."

  DALY — the 4% attention threshold (the adverse trip wire):
    "a forthcoming AER paper … argues that consumers' attention to inflation …
     impacts inflation itself … this author actually estimates that the
     threshold where people's attention focuses on inflation is 4%. And we're
     very close to 4%. We are 3.9 on average and 3.7 of late."
    → A near-breached threshold on HEADLINE experience.

  → The two are not contradictory — they measure shock-stripped core versus
    lived headline. But they key the September decision to different series. The
    question to put to the committee: which one is the reaction function
    actually running on?
  → [UNVERIFIED — DO NOT SCORE] Media reporting suggests Musalem also gave an
    underlying-inflation range (2.5–3.0%) and Schmid also referenced underlying
    inflation being too high. If confirmed on recovery, "underlying inflation"
    with an explicit numeric range becomes the cycle's fastest-spreading
    analytical frame and moves straight to COMMITTEE-WIDE. Recover both texts
    before drawing that conclusion.

THE AI BUILDOUT: ONE PHENOMENON, THREE INCOMPATIBLE READINGS IN THREE DAYS
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  COOK (Aug 5) — DISINFLATIONARY. Her third named disinflation channel: "some of
    the recent price pressure in goods is due to the relative demand shift from
    the AI buildout … As supply chains adjust and sector-specific efficiency
    gains accrue, I believe some of the inflationary pressure coming from the AI
    buildout will ease."
    ⚠ This is a REVERSAL. On July 15 the AI buildout was her reason #1 that
    risks were "strongly weighted toward higher inflation" — "does not show
    signs of slowing … $1.5 trillion in data center plans, only a small portion
    of which has been realized." Twenty-one days later it changed sides. No
    obvious data forces this; the July 28–29 meeting sits between.

  PAULSON (Aug 4) — REASON POLICY MAY NOT BE RESTRICTIVE ENOUGH. Weighs "the AI
    buildout and the investment boom. Stock markets have been pretty robust"
    against her mildly-restrictive assessment.

  DALY (Aug 6) — EXPLICITLY UNRESOLVED, and she is the one closest to the
    industry: "it remains an open question whether this is a relative demand
    shock and just changes the relative price of technology equipment … or if
    it's something broader … because it's competing for resources in a way that
    spills over into overall inflation." She adds a mechanism nobody else has:
    resource crowding — "what do the other businesses feel like they're not
    getting or they're paying more for because the AI investment boom is taking
    resources that they would otherwise have access to."

  → Three voting-relevant officials, three signs, seventy-two hours. Daly's
    agnosticism is the most informed position on record given the SF district's
    line to the AI firms. Cook's resolution is the least supported and is the
    top analyst flag of the cycle.
  → [UNVERIFIED — DO NOT SCORE] Schmid is reported to have said AI investment is
    pushing up inflation "in a way the Fed can't ignore." If confirmed, that is a
    fourth reading and makes the AI channel the most contested object on the
    committee.

THE LOOK-THROUGH DOCTRINE — AN OPEN, NAMED EXCHANGE
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The July 31 scored files flagged Kashkari's successive-shocks argument as the
phrase most likely to be contested. It was contested within four days, in
public, by name.

  KASHKARI (Jul 31 written, restated Aug 5): "traditional economic analysis says
    if there's a one-time supply shock, monetary policy should just let that flow
    through … But now we've had a series of successive supply shocks over four or
    five years." Escalated on Aug 5 to a sourced historical charge: "I went back
    to the transcripts from the 1970s. In the 1970s, the Burns Fed also diagnosed
    the high inflation as a series of successive supply shocks … I don't think
    anybody would look back and say, 'Boy, the Burns Fed was right to just look
    through all those supply shocks.'" Hedged: "this is not the 1970s."

  PAULSON (Aug 4), asked about Kashkari's position BY NAME: "I have definitely
    been of the view that you should look through supply shocks. I think the June
    PCE inflation data kind of validate that … when we had the lull in the
    conflict in the Middle East, oil prices came down, we got inflation relief.
    If I had tried to affect that through monetary policy, I would have been too
    late."
    → A TIMING argument, not a level argument. She does not say the shocks are
      small; she says policy cannot arrive when they do. Bounded by second-round
      effects: "that's where monetary policy has a real role to play, to keep
      those shocks in their lane."

  DALY (Aug 6) states Paulson's doctrine as textbook — "monetary policy acts
    with a lag, 12 to 18 months … by the time you've raised the rate … you
    haven't affected the shock dynamic, but you've definitely affected the
    economy and unnecessarily so" — and then asks the question Paulson does not:
    does it survive THREE OVERLAPPING shocks? "before the tariff shock is fully
    through the economy, the oil shock comes … And then before that's fully
    dissipated, the AI shock comes … are they going to have a compounding
    effect? … greater than the sum of its parts."

  → Daly's compounding hypothesis is Kashkari's argument rebuilt analytically
    rather than by analogy, and left OPEN rather than asserted. That makes it
    adoptable by majority members in a way Kashkari's version is not — it does
    not require conceding the Burns charge.
  → Language cycle stage: EMERGING and CONTESTED. This is the formulation most
    likely to appear in the July 28–29 minutes (Aug 19), and how the minutes
    characterise it — a few / several / many participants — is the highest-value
    scheduled read before Jackson Hole.

  ⚠ KASHKARI'S ATTRIBUTION SHIFT, easily missed: on Aug 5 he SUBORDINATED
  demand — "I don't think most of the inflation that we've experienced is
  because of economic growth and robust demand. I think most of the inflation
  has been because of these successive supply shocks. There is a demand element
  on top of it as well." His July 31 statement had named data centers as "a new
  demand element." This separates him from Hammack and Logan, who argue the
  residual IS demand. The three dissenters voted identically for materially
  different reasons — the bloc is less likely to act as a unit in September than
  the simultaneous July 31 statements implied.
